# Training Fleet

The **Training Fleet** is the set of small consumer DJI aircraft recommended for AERO 100, where the job is getting students comfortable on the sticks, not producing data. The December 2025 equipment memo recommends a mixed fleet of DJI Mini 3 and DJI Air 3 Fly More combos, three of each, chosen because consumer DJI aircraft are inexpensive enough to crash, unintimidating for new pilots, and use the same control scheme as the enterprise aircraft students graduate to [1]. The whole package, aircraft plus chargers, power station, case, landing pads, and course materials, comes to about $10,400 with tax [1].

## DJI Mini 3

The Mini 3 is the budget anchor: about $719 per Fly More combo as of the December 2025 memo [1]. It weighs 248 g including battery and microSD card, flies up to 38 minutes on its 18.1 Wh, 2,453 mAh battery, and handles wind to 10.7 m/s, level 5 [2]. The camera is a 12 MP 1/1.3-inch sensor, modest but irrelevant for a stick-skills course. Its light weight is its charm and its limitation. Under 250 g it escapes registration for purely recreational flying, though aircraft flown under Part 107, which is how a college course operates, must be registered regardless of weight, a distinction covered in [Drone Registration](../regulations/drone-registration.md); registration is five dollars, so the administrative savings the memo cites is real but small [1]. In wind the Mini 3 works harder and drifts more than heavier aircraft, which is honestly useful for teaching wind awareness, and its low mass minimizes consequence when a student flies it into something.

## DJI Air 3

The Air 3, about $1,939 per Fly More combo as of the memo [1], is the preferred primary trainer if budget allows. At 720 g it must be registered, flies up to 46 minutes on a 62.6 Wh, 4,241 mAh battery, and resists wind to 12 m/s, the same rating as the Matrice 4 series [3]. It carries dual 48 MP cameras, a wide and a 3x medium telephoto, and omnidirectional obstacle sensing, the most complete collision avoidance in its class, which matters when six student aircraft share a field [1] [3]. It feels more substantial in the air, holds position better, and gives instructors a second aircraft personality to contrast with the Mini 3, reinforcing the memo's point that skills transfer across airframes.

## Controllers, accounts, and Remote ID

Both combos are specified with DJI's controllers with built-in screens, the DJI RC for the Mini 3 and the RC 2 for the Air 3. The memo is emphatic that separate student phones are a mess: a self-contained controller means a kit that powers on and flies with nothing to pair, update, or borrow [1]. Both models broadcast standard Remote ID out of the box, though the sub-250 g Mini 3 is only required to broadcast when operating under Part 107, per [Remote Identification](../regulations/remote-identification.md). Fleet setup requires deciding between one master DJI account or per-aircraft email aliases; the planning memo leans toward whichever makes checkout simplest, and every drone gets a name and a label [1] [4].

## Fleet logistics

The memo's fleet logic: labs are hands-on and one instructor can safely supervise three to six concurrent flights, so six aircraft with three batteries each covers a full lab section with rotation [1]. Each Fly More combo's three batteries buy roughly 90 minutes of realistic flight time per aircraft, and the [field charging setup](field-power-and-charging.md), an EcoFlow power station with parallel charging hubs, keeps batteries cycling through a three-hour lab. A single Pelican-style case (roughly $150 to $300) transports the entire fleet with controllers and batteries in one box; landing pads (about $20 each) give clean takeoff surfaces and double as exercise targets; a label maker (about $45) keeps six identical aircraft and controllers sorted [1]. About $250 is budgeted for materials to build the NIST Open Test Lanes apparatus, the standardized proficiency course described in [Flight Proficiency and Practice Exercises](../topics/flight-proficiency-and-practice-exercises.md) [1].

## The NDAA caveat

This fleet is DJI, and the program's advisors keep asking for non-Chinese platforms. The honest arithmetic: six DJI trainers cost less than $9,000, while the cheapest credible NDAA-compliant trainer costs about $7,000 per aircraft, so a compliant fleet of six would consume most of the entire equipment budget [5]. The April 2026 proposal splits the difference by planning three to four compliant trainers at $5,000 to $7,000 each instead of six to eight identical DJI aircraft [6]. Whatever the mix, the training fleet must be purchased with non-federal money if it is DJI, and the compliant options are weighed in [NDAA-Compliant Platform Options](ndaa-compliant-platform-options.md). A second caveat is sourcing: DJI consumer aircraft have been increasingly difficult to buy in the United States since the FCC covered-list action in late 2025, which is why the memo recommends the proven, currently purchasable Mini 3 and Air 3 rather than newer models [1] [5].

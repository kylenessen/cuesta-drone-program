# Flame Wheel Budget Build

The **Flame Wheel budget build** is a researched low-cost alternative configuration for the [AERO 100 Student-Built Drone](aero-100-student-built-drone.md), adapted from Hoarder Sam's October 2025 video "I Built a $150 Autonomous Drone" [1]. Where the Aether path builds a modern 4 inch FPV-class quad around an integrated stack, this path reaches back a decade to the parts ecosystem that hobbyists used before ready-to-fly drones existed: a DJI Flame Wheel clone frame, airplane motors with bullet connectors, five-dollar airplane speed controllers, and an RC airplane flight controller whose servo-style headers accept those speed controllers without custom wiring. The result is a GPS quadcopter that loiters, returns home, and flies waypoint missions for roughly $150 to $180 per aircraft, flies more than twenty minutes on a cheap RC-car battery, and lifts up to two pounds of payload. The pedagogical claim, which the research below supports, is that the old parts teach better than the new ones precisely because nothing is integrated: every connection between battery, distribution board, speed controller, motor, and flight controller is a wire the student routed and a joint the student soldered. This page records the source build, the ArduPilot verification, the sizing decision, a July 2026 reference bill of materials, video system options, and the comparison with the Aether path. Like its sibling page, it documents a researched proposal, not a purchase.

## The source build

The video documents a complete build in the classic style [1]. The frame is an F450 Flame Wheel clone kit, about $20 with the power distribution board integrated into the frame plates and tall landing gear included. The motors are 2212-class airplane motors at $5 each, mounted with prop collets rather than lock nuts; the creator used 1400KV and recommends 1200KV or lower with 8x4.5 slow-fly props. The speed controllers are generic 40 amp airplane units, also $5 each, with servo connectors on their signal leads. The flight controller is the SpeedyBee F405 Wing Mini, a $40 board sold for RC airplanes, chosen exactly because its rows of servo headers let the airplane speed controllers plug straight in. An HGLRC M100-5883 GPS and compass ($20), a 2.4GHz ExpressLRS receiver ($10), and a 3S 5000mAh RC-car battery ($30) complete the aircraft, which runs iNav 8, holds GPS position, and flies waypoint missions drawn in iNav Configurator. There is no camera; the aircraft flies line of sight. A $5 servo-operated release and a $5 MG995 servo give it a payload-drop party trick that is really a payload-systems lesson. The creator's claimed total is $150; his own published parts sheet sums closer to $165 to $175 once four motors and four speed controllers are counted, which is worth knowing when the number is quoted [1].

The design philosophy matters more than the parts list. The build deliberately pairs large slow propellers with small cheap motors for efficiency, the same disc-loading logic that lets a 250 gram DJI Mini fly half an hour, and it frames the aircraft as an experimental payload platform, "a drone with a job," rather than an FPV sport machine. That framing matches the program's mapping identity better than the racing-derived hardware the FPV market centers on.

## Why the old parts teach

The teaching value the video demonstrates is a direct consequence of using pre-integration hardware. The motors arrive with bullet connectors, and motor direction is set physically: a brushless motor reverses when any two of its three phase wires are swapped, so students discover three-phase commutation with their fingers instead of a firmware checkbox. The speed controllers are dumb PWM devices, so a $2 servo tester becomes a diagnostic instrument, and the video itself hits a dead motor on the bench and walks through fault isolation by substitution, exactly the troubleshooting habit a maintenance curriculum wants. The airplane speed controllers each carry a battery eliminator circuit that must be depinned before four of them share one flight controller, which forces a concrete lesson in power architecture: what a BEC is, why parallel five-volt regulators fight, and where the flight controller actually gets its power. Power distribution is soldered by hand onto the frame's distribution board, and the battery current path runs through the flight controller's shunt, so current sensing stops being abstract. None of this happens on a modern integrated stack, where the four speed controllers, the distribution board, and the regulators are one opaque four-in-one board and motor direction is a configurator toggle. The integrated stack builds a cleaner aircraft; the exposed one builds a clearer mental model, which is the pivot's stated goal.

## ArduPilot runs on the same hardware

The video uses iNav, but the firmware research from the sibling page carries over: this exact board is on ArduPilot's supported list. The SpeedyBee F405 Wing Mini shares the `SpeedyBeeF405WING` firmware target with its full-size sibling, has its own page in the Copter documentation, and the firmware server carries stable ArduCopter builds for it (4.6.3 as of this research) [2][3]. The build's feature list was checked directly: Loiter, position hold, return-to-launch, Auto waypoint missions, and geofencing are all compiled in, and what the 1MB flash strips is niche (Lua scripting, follow mode, precision landing) [4]. The cheap PWM speed controllers are not a compromise for ArduPilot either, since normal PWM is Copter's default motor protocol [5]. The honest costs of PWM are a required ESC calibration step, itself a worthwhile lab, and the absence of per-motor telemetry [6]. Airplane speed controllers also want their brake and low-voltage-cutoff options disabled, and no-name units should be bench-checked at multirotor update rates before fleet purchase. ExpressLRS telemetry over CRSF, including the Yaapu telemetry script on the transmitter, is supported on this target [7]. One naming trap: the SpeedyBee F405 Mini from the Aether bill of materials is a different racing-style board with a different firmware target; this is the F405 **Wing** Mini.

So the working direction from the sibling page, ArduPilot first with iNav as fallback, holds unchanged on this hardware. iNav 8 does fly waypoint missions competently on this board, up to 120 waypoints drawn in its configurator's mission tab [8], but Mission Planner and MAVLink remain the stack with employment value, and the same board runs both, so the video's firmware choice is a convenience, not a constraint.

## Size, and the 330 millimeter answer

Kyle's size ceiling for the student build is the DJI Phantom. A Phantom 4 is 350mm motor-to-motor on 9.4 inch props, about 590mm tip to tip. The F450 on 8 inch props is about 650mm tip to tip, roughly ten percent over the ceiling, and on the 10 inch props these kits usually ship with it exceeds it by twenty percent. The fix costs nothing: the same ecosystem includes the F330 Flame Wheel and its clones, 330mm frames that run at about 530mm tip to tip on the same 8x4.5 props, ten percent smaller than the Phantom. The F330 is not an improvisation; DJI's own manual specced it for exactly this motor class, 2212 stators around 920KV on 8045 props at 600 to 1200 grams takeoff weight, and an archived official ArduPilot F330 build guide exists [9][10]. Clone F330 frames run $11 at US hobby retailers and a few dollars from China [11]. The tradeoff is interior room: the F450's roomy center bay for battery and payload shrinks, and the landing-gear and dropper accessories are F450-standard. Either way the motor purchase should be 920KV to 1000KV rather than the video's 1400KV, which the creator himself flags as too hot. Both frames keep the crash economics that make this class attractive: replacement injection-molded arms cost a couple of dollars each and bolt on in minutes, which is arguably even more classroom-friendly than reprinting an Aether unibody overnight.

## Reference bill of materials

Prices are July 2026, one aircraft, no video system, mixing the cheapest substantiated source per line (AliExpress-level China-direct where meaningful, US retail where the gap has closed) [11][12][13][14].

| Part | Example | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Frame kit with integrated PDB | F330 or F450 clone, landing gear included | $15 |
| Motors, set of 4 plus a spare | 2212 class, 920 to 1000KV | $20 |
| Speed controllers, 4 plus a spare | generic 30A airplane, SimonK/BLHeli | $25 |
| Propellers | 8045 slow-fly, 8 pairs | $10 |
| Flight controller | SpeedyBee F405 Wing Mini | $43 |
| GPS and compass | HGLRC M100-5883 | $20 |
| Radio receiver | ExpressLRS 2.4GHz, BetaFPV Lite class | $11 |
| Battery | CNHL 3S 5000mAh, US warehouse | $24 |
| Wiring and small parts | XT60 pigtails, 14AWG wire, nylon standoffs | $10 |
| Printed mounting plates | FC plate and GPS plate, PETG | $1 |

The total is about $180 as specced, and patient AliExpress sourcing of the frame, motors, speed controllers, props, and GPS brings it to roughly $150, which ties the video's number with better motor choices; his October 2025 sheet is honest AliExpress pricing and there is no large margin left under it. The number can be pushed to about $135 by substituting a $10 CNHL 2200mAh pack, at the cost of most of the endurance headline. Beating the number is therefore possible but not the point; the point is that the price is half the Aether path's $315 for a more capable payload platform. Two 2026 sourcing realities from this research: US tariff surcharges (SpeedyBee's direct store now adds a 54 percent prepaid tariff at checkout) have narrowed the AliExpress advantage to the point that RaceDayQuads and eBay-US are often within a few dollars, with motors and props the remaining genuinely-cheap AliExpress wins; and lithium batteries should simply be bought domestic, since hazmat shipping keeps AliExpress packs at or above the $24 CNHL US-warehouse price [13]. At roughly a kilogram takeoff weight this aircraft is well over the 250 gram line, so registration applies and a Remote ID module (about $35, omitted at a FRIA) belongs in any fleet budget, the same compliance-as-curriculum position the sibling page records. The one-time ground kit (ExpressLRS radio, charger, smoke stopper, soldering gear) is unchanged from the sibling page at about $180, with the note that the RadioMaster Pocket's US street price has drifted from $65 toward $85 under the same tariffs [15].

## Video options

The aircraft needs no camera for the course goal, and the video's own build flies without one. If video is added, analog remains decisively cheaper per aircraft: a Caddx Ant class camera plus a budget transmitter runs $30 to $45 at US retail, an all-in-one camera-transmitter unit runs about $26 to $30, and AliExpress-level equivalents run near $20 [16]. Digital air units run $89 to $110 (Walksnail Avatar Lite, HDZero Whoop, DJI O4) with goggles from $199, a delta of $50 to $70 per aircraft plus $60 to $120 on the ground side [16]. The one interesting exception is the new Walksnail Ascent Lite system, a $36 to $40 digital air unit with $149 goggles, which erases most of the digital premium in exchange for ecosystem lock-in; worth watching, not yet worth committing a fleet to. A wrinkle this research surfaced: 2026 tariffs have inflated the classic cheap analog goggles (the Eachine EV800D now runs $140 to $160 US where it was long $90), so the analog ground station should be priced at about $110 (FatShark Echo class) rather than from memory. The practical answer for a first semester is the video's answer: fly line of sight, keep the flight controller's camera pads empty, and let analog FPV be a late-semester elective add at about $30 per aircraft plus one shared ground station.

## Payload as capstone

The $10 payload drop mechanism deserves attention beyond its entertainment value. It exercises the servo output path (a mixer rule in iNav, a gripper or servo function in ArduPilot), it demonstrates payload physics viscerally (the video documents a two-pound bottle on a long string driving the aircraft into pendulum oscillation, and the fix, shortening the string, is a control-theory lesson disguised as rigging), and it connects the trainer to the delivery and agricultural applications the program's advanced courses discuss. The two-pound capacity also means the aircraft can lift a small action camera or survey camera, which opens a low-stakes bridge to the program's mapping curriculum: a student-built aircraft flying a waypoint lawnmower pattern with a $30 camera is a photogrammetry lab that risks nothing the program cannot afford to crash.

## Against the Aether path

The two builds are not competitors so much as answers to different constraints, and the honest comparison is worth recording. The Flame Wheel build is roughly half the per-aircraft price ($150 to $180 against $315), flies twice as long (twenty-plus minutes against about twelve), lifts real payload, and teaches wiring and power architecture that the integrated stack hides; its crash spares are $2 bolt-on arms. The Aether build is a quarter of the weight (under 300 grams against about a kilogram), which matters for both the harmlessness goal and the registration question, it flies indoors and in a netted gym where the Flame Wheel needs a field, and it produces an aircraft in the modern FPV idiom students will recognize from the hobby. The kinetic energy difference is the real safety consideration: docility can be configured in software on either aircraft, but mass cannot. A plausible synthesis rather than a choice: the Flame Wheel class as the shared, instructor-maintained platform for payload and mission labs (a few aircraft, group-built), and a smaller class for the one-per-student build-and-crash trainer. The instructor prototype settles this cheaply, since the electronics core here (flight controller, GPS, receiver) is about $75 and every conclusion transfers.

## Open questions and next steps

The bench questions before any fleet purchase: whether the no-name speed controllers hold up at ArduPilot's default 490Hz update rate or need `RC_SPEED` lowered, how 920KV against 1000KV motors fly on 8 inch props on the 330 frame, and whether the F330's smaller bay fits the 5000mAh pack that makes the endurance number. The curriculum questions: whether this build replaces the Aether as the primary student aircraft or complements it as the payload platform, whether the capstone payload is the dropper or a camera, and whether one aircraft per student or per pair is the right ratio at this price. The compliance question is unchanged from the sibling page: registration and Remote ID are curriculum content at this weight, and a FRIA would waive the module cost. An instructor prototype in the video's exact configuration, then reflashed to ArduPilot, is the obvious first step and costs under $200 to answer every question above.

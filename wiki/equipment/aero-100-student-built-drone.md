# AERO 100 Student-Built Drone

The **AERO 100 Student-Built Drone** is the semester build at the center of the basic operator course: students build a small GPS-capable quadcopter and fly it through the same test lane that carries the course flight capstone. The design target is a drone that mimics a commercial platform, meaning it holds position on GPS, returns to home on command, and flies basic autonomous waypoints, while staying small, cheap to crash, and relatively harmless. The pedagogical bet is that building the aircraft is the fastest way to learn how drones work and, just as important, how they fail: GPS loss, compass calibration, failsafe and return-to-home behavior, telemetry, tuning, and battery management all become hands-on rather than lecture material. The explicit non-goal is first-person-view racing or freestyle athleticism. The FPV hobby supplies the parts ecosystem, since its frames, motors, controllers, and batteries are the open commodity hardware market, but the flying style and the video gear are optional, and the firmware choice is what separates a docile trainer from a race drone.

This page records the build research: what the goal requires, the training progression the build enables, the candidate Aether 4 printed airframe, the carbon-frame alternative and the instructor prototype path, a reference bill of materials, crash economics, the weight-class decision, ownership models, and safety. The build began as a researched proposal, and Kyle and Ron agreed in July 2026 to make it a central feature of AERO 100. The advisory-facing statement of the course lives in the repository at `curriculum-content/aero-100-advisory-outline.md`. The parts list and fleet purchases remain open, and the consumer DJI [Training Fleet](training-fleet.md) flies alongside the build as the trainer and camera-assessment aircraft. The firmware background is in [Flight Controller Firmware](../topics/flight-controller-firmware.md).

## What the goal requires

Mimicking a commercial platform sets a short hardware list beyond a basic drone: a GPS receiver and compass for position hold and return-to-home, a barometer for altitude hold, a radio receiver, and firmware that can fly waypoint missions. The firmware choice is the pivotal one, developed in [Flight Controller Firmware](../topics/flight-controller-firmware.md), and the research behind it has moved. Earlier planning treated ArduPilot as requiring heavier, costlier Pixhawk-class hardware, with iNav as the cheap-hardware compromise. ArduPilot's supported-hardware list now includes a long roster of FPV-class boards, among them the 20x20mm SpeedyBee F405 Mini stack, with the caveat that F4-generation chips lack the flash for some optional features [1]. The navigation core the course needs, position hold, altitude hold, return-to-home, waypoint missions, and geofencing, fits on those boards. The consequence is that the commercial-autopilot behavior no longer carries a cost premium over the hobby-navigation tier, so the working direction is ArduPilot on FPV-class hardware, with iNav as the fallback if ArduPilot setup proves too heavy for the classroom and Betaflight set aside entirely. ArduPilot also carries the stronger employment argument, because Mission Planner, MAVLink telemetry, and log-based diagnosis are the same stack behind the professional non-DJI platforms the advanced courses point toward.

## Training progression the build enables

The build supports a two-track semester rather than a build-then-fly sequence. Flight starts in the first weeks on a simulator and on a pre-built, pre-tuned reference aircraft flown in GPS-assisted mode, so students who have never flown get an early win and stick time accumulates all semester. The build runs alongside, and each student aircraft's maiden flight lands mid-semester in the assisted modes, with modes unlocking progressively toward the capstone documented in [Flight Proficiency and Practice Exercises](../topics/flight-proficiency-and-practice-exercises.md) and [NIST Open Test Lane](../exams/nist-open-test-lane.md).

The mode ladder is built into the autopilot. In Loiter the aircraft parks itself when the sticks are released and flies much like a consumer DJI in normal mode. Altitude hold removes the position assist but keeps the height. Stabilize leaves only self-leveling, and manual acro is the earned endpoint for students who want it. ArduPilot exposes per-aircraft limits on maximum lean angle, Loiter speed and acceleration, and a geofence the aircraft cannot leave, so the same airframe can be configured as a docile trainer for one student and a full-authority machine for another, reversibly. Docility comes from software limits rather than from underpowering the aircraft, which matters because a weak drone cannot arrest a botched descent, while a limited strong one keeps its recovery thrust in reserve.

Manual-mode competence is job preparation, not hobby athleticism. GPS-assisted flight fails in exactly the environments the program's graduates will work in, because compasses corrupt and GPS multipaths near steel and energized infrastructure, as documented in [Flying Near Energized Infrastructure](../topics/flying-near-energized-infrastructure.md), and commercial aircraft respond by silently dropping into unassisted drift modes. A pilot who has flown the whole mode ladder recognizes and flies through that failure. A side effect of the progression is calibrated respect for commercial platforms: students who have chased a compass calibration or a vibration problem understand what the integrated aircraft is doing for them.

## Candidate airframe: the Aether 4

The leading airframe candidate is the BM Aether 4, a fully 3D-printed unibody frame for 4 and 4.5 inch propellers published on MakerWorld in July 2024 [2]. It prints in one piece with no frame assembly, weighs about 40 grams, and one roll of filament yields roughly twenty frames, which puts the marginal cost of a crashed frame near three dollars and an overnight print. It takes the standard 20x20mm stack, 1404 to 2004 motors on 9mm or 12mm bolt patterns, and props up to 4.5 inches, and the designer publishes a GPS-holder accessory and the original CAD in Onshape [2]. The designer's own sub-250-gram setup cruises about twelve minutes on a 4S 850mAh pack, and builder comments document GPS-equipped builds in carbon-reinforced PETG flying well on stock tune [2]. For a classroom, the crash economics are the headline: a broken frame stops being a budget event and becomes a normal lab occurrence with a repair lesson attached.

The honest caveats come from the designer's own guidance [2]. The frame prints without infill in a rigid filament, PETG-CF or PLA-CF over plain PLA for anything beyond gentle flying, and it is sensitive to vibration, which matters more under ArduPilot than under the Betaflight tune the designer documents, because ArduPilot's state estimation is picky about vibration. Heat under the motors softens the plastic and backs motor screws out, which the designer calls the number one cause of dirty flight, so threadlocker and a motor-screw check belong on the preflight list, a habit worth teaching anyway. A builder comment reports 1404 motors running hot when pushed with 4 inch props, so the motor spec should favor adequately sized low-KV motors, in the 1504 to 2004 range, over the smallest motor swinging the biggest prop [2]. The frame handles knocks but breaks in a serious crash, which the reprint economics absorb. One administrative caveat: the model moved to a MakerWorld-exclusive license in October 2024 that bars redistribution and requires the designer's authorization for commercial production [2]. Printing for the program's own classroom use appears within the license, but a fleet decision should include a courtesy contact with the designer.

## Carbon alternative and the prototype path

A conventional carbon fiber frame in the same class costs roughly $25 to $45 at mid-2026 retail, with popular 4 inch long-range examples including the Flywoo Explorer LR4, the GEPRC Crocodile Baby, and the open-source TBS Source One family, all taking the same electronics. Carbon is stiffer, which means better vibration behavior, and it survives crashes that break a printed unibody. The tradeoffs are assembly from plates and standoffs, replacement arms that ship rather than print, and a camera cage that sits empty on a cameraless build. Reference aircraft prove the 4 inch GPS class works: the Flywoo Explorer LR4 ships as a sub-250-gram GPS quad with return-to-home [3], and the documented Mayfly V2.0 is the same idea scratch-built [4]. At the opposite end, the turnkey Holybro X500 development kit remains the university-standard Pixhawk platform [5], but it is set aside as the primary classroom aircraft because a larger, costlier machine defeats the crash-friendly design goal, while remaining the natural reference for later-course autopilot work.

The prototype path changes one variable at a time. The instructor build starts on a carbon frame, where the vibration variable is smallest, and gets ArduPilot Loiter working with clean logs as the baseline. The same electronics then swap onto a printed Aether 4, an afternoon of work, and the two frames fly the same missions. Any difference in the vibration and tuning logs is then attributable to the frame alone, which is precisely the evidence the classroom-fleet decision needs and a defensible answer to the committee question of whether 3D-printed airframes are classroom-worthy.

## The Flame Wheel budget alternative

A second researched path, documented separately in [Flame Wheel Budget Build](flame-wheel-budget-build.md), reaches the same goal from the opposite end of the parts market. Adapted from a documented $150 build video, it pairs a DJI Flame Wheel clone frame (F330 or F450) with $5 airplane motors and speed controllers and an RC airplane flight controller whose servo headers accept them directly, and it runs the same ArduPilot-or-iNav firmware, GPS, and ExpressLRS link as the build below. It lands at roughly half this page's per-aircraft price, flies twice as long, and lifts real payload, at the cost of being a kilogram-class aircraft that is decisively over the 250 gram line and too large for indoor flying. Its distinct teaching claim is that the pre-integration hardware exposes what the integrated stack hides: hand-soldered power distribution, motor direction set by swapping phase wires, and speed controllers whose battery eliminator circuits must be understood before they can share a flight controller. The two paths are compared honestly on that page; the short version is that they answer different constraints and a cheap instructor prototype settles both.

## Reference bill of materials

Prices are July 2026 retail, approximate, for one aircraft with no video system.

| Part | Example | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Frame, printed | Aether 4 in PETG-CF, 3 walls, no infill | $3 |
| Frame hardware | M2 shoulder screws per the designer's list | $3 |
| Flight controller and ESC stack | SpeedyBee F405 Mini BLS 35A, 20x20mm | $65 |
| Motors, set of 4 | 2004-class, roughly 1700KV, 4S | $65 |
| Propellers | Gemfan 4525 two-blade, ten sets | $20 |
| GPS and compass | HGLRC M100-5883 | $20 |
| Radio receiver | ExpressLRS, RadioMaster RP1 or similar | $17 |
| Lost-aircraft buzzer | ViFly Finder 2, self-powered | $16 |
| Batteries, 3 | 4S 850mAh LiPo | $60 |
| Remote ID module | Holybro or BlueMark, omit at a FRIA | $35 |
| Wiring and small parts | XT30 pigtail, strap, heatshrink | $10 |

The total is about $315 as specced, of which the durable electronics core, meaning stack, motors, GPS, receiver, and buzzer, is about $185, with the balance in consumables and compliance. The stack sells for $64.99 direct from SpeedyBee [6] but was listed at $125.99 on Amazon in July 2026 [7], so sourcing from specialty retailers roughly halves that line. The GPS ran $19.99 on Amazon [8]. Two checks before ordering: the motor bolt pattern must match the frame's 9mm or 12mm mounts, and the battery, ESC rating, and motor KV must all agree on 4S. The cell count is set by the motor windings, not by preference. Motors in this class are wound for 4S to 6S, single-cell packs belong to indoor micro-whoops, and gentleness is achieved in software rather than by starving the powertrain of voltage. If ArduPilot's F4 flash limits ever bite, 20x20mm H743 stacks exist at roughly twice the stack price and lift every limitation [1].

The ground kit is a one-time purchase of about $180: a RadioMaster Pocket ExpressLRS radio near $65, a charger near $55, a LiPo-safe charging bag, a smoke stopper, the inline fuse that turns a first-power-up wiring fault into a lesson instead of a dead stack, and a soldering iron if none is on hand. DJI controllers cannot be reused, since they speak DJI's proprietary link and bind only to DJI aircraft. One ExpressLRS radio binds to any number of aircraft and doubles as the controller for the flight simulators, and ExpressLRS can carry MAVLink telemetry to a laptop running Mission Planner, so the ground station may need no extra radio.

## Crash economics and consumables

The purchase price and the cost of flying it are different numbers. A typical training crash costs propellers at a dollar or two each and sometimes the frame at three dollars of filament. The electronics ride protected in the middle of the aircraft and rarely die from impact. Motors occasionally take a bent shaft or scraped bell on hard surfaces but usually survive grass. When electronics do die it is from wiring faults and shorts, a build-quality problem the smoke stopper mostly prevents, not a crash problem. Batteries are wear items that age over charge cycles or die by puncture. The recurring semester cost is therefore filament, propellers, and replacement packs, while the electronics carry over year to year. For scale, a whole ten-aircraft trainer fleet prices near a single enterprise mapping aircraft, and students can fly the trainers without the anxiety that grounds expensive platforms.

## Kit versus scratch for a classroom

The learning value and the reliability risk pull in opposite directions. Scratch-building from a parts pile is where the real teaching lives, soldering, wiring, flashing firmware, calibration, and debugging why an aircraft will not arm, which is exactly the discovery through building the pivot is after. The risk is time and yield: a from-scratch build can consume weeks and produce dead boards, which is a serious hazard when the instructor is also learning. A common middle path in college programs is a parts list that the college pre-vets and buys in bulk, so the build is reproducible year to year, while students still perform all assembly and configuration. Soldering itself is defensible workforce content rather than a burden, and if a classroom constraint ever rules it out, the build can be respecced around plug-connector components at some cost in part selection. Community-college precedents for build-it-yourself drone courses exist to borrow from, including programs at Germanna and Fullerton and the Carnegie Mellon CoDrone curriculum.

## Weight class and what it teaches

The build's weight is a deliberate curriculum lever rather than a fixed constraint. An aircraft kept under 250 grams is exempt from FAA registration and from Remote Identification, while one at 250 grams or more triggers both [9]. Because the flight capstone scores the same at any weight, as noted in [NIST Open Test Lane](../exams/nist-open-test-lane.md), the program can choose the weight class for its teaching value. An Aether-class build carrying GPS, three-battery flexibility, and a Remote ID module will likely land modestly above 250 grams, and the current direction treats that as acceptable and even useful: a build at or above the line pulls [Drone Registration](../regulations/drone-registration.md) and [Remote Identification](../regulations/remote-identification.md) into the course as compliance tasks students actually perform, turning two regulatory topics into lived procedure. The tradeoff is that a heavier aircraft is less harmless and loses the simplicity of the unregistered class.

## Video as a deferred option

The aircraft needs no camera to meet the course goal, and omitting the video system removes both the largest cost block and the FPV-culture center of gravity. The choice stays open cheaply: the reference stack carries dedicated camera and video-transmitter pads and a 9 volt supply for them [6], so analog video is a later add of roughly $50 on the aircraft, camera, transmitter, and antenna, plus shared goggles on the ground. Builder comments on the Aether document analog camera installations [2]. That keeps first-person flying available as an elective unit late in the course, an experiment students run on aircraft they built, without making it the spine of the class.

## Ownership models

Two models were raised, and the parts choices suit both. In the first, Cuesta owns a fleet: the college buys a pile of parts, students build and later disassemble the aircraft, and the parts are reused the following year. This favors durable, standardized, repairable components and a fixed bill of materials. In the second, students buy their own parts and keep the finished drone, which favors low per-student cost and clear individual sourcing. The pre-vetted-parts-list approach serves either model. A hybrid worth noting: the ExpressLRS radio is the natural student-owned item under either model, priced like a textbook, usable with the simulators at home, and theirs for every aircraft they ever fly afterward.

## Safety

Keeping the aircraft harmless rests on a few levers. Staying small keeps prop energy low, propeller guards or ducts reduce injury and damage risk further, and early manual-flight weeks flown in a net enclosure or gymnasium contain the roughest attempts. Lithium-polymer battery handling is its own discipline, covered in [Battery Fundamentals and Safety](../topics/battery-fundamentals-and-safety.md), and a build course adds charging-bag, storage-voltage, and fireproof-charging-area practice to it. These habits are themselves teachable content that transfers directly to the enterprise aircraft.

## Open questions and next steps

The near-term step is an instructor prototype over summer 2026, built on the carbon-then-Aether path above, before any classroom commitment. The open decisions it feeds: the per-aircraft budget and fleet size, including whether students build individually or in pairs; the motor spec, where the docility-versus-headroom balance between 1504 and 2004 class motors is best settled by flight test; whether cheap ducted indoor whoops are worth adding for first stick time; and the license contact with the Aether designer before fleet printing. A longer-term idea recorded here is a Cuesta-designed teaching frame, since in this ecosystem designing a drone mostly means designing a frame around standardized commodity electronics. A teaching frame could make choices hobby frames do not, such as modular bolt-on arms that reprint in minutes, printed prop-guard mounts, a proper GPS mast, a Remote ID bay, and a registration-number plate, and the Aether's published Onshape design and the open-source Source One CAD are the study references. That design work should wait for a semester of real crash data.

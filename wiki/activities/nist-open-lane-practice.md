# NIST Open Lane Practice Sessions

**NIST Open Lane Practice Sessions** are the recurring lab that carries AERO 100's flight proficiency outcome. The course's approved flight exam is the NIST Open Test Lane Level 2 course flown within the NIST-prescribed 25 minute time limit [1], and the honest way to prepare students for a standard test method is to fly the actual apparatus, on a schedule, with scores recorded from the first week. The lane is cheap to build, fast to set up, and scores itself through the targets, so the same session design works as practice in week four and as the capstone exam in week fifteen. The background on what the tests measure and why they exist lives in [Flight Proficiency and Practice Exercises](../topics/flight-proficiency-and-practice-exercises.md); this page is the runnable session.

## What it teaches

The lane exercises nearly every line of the approved outline's UAV Flight Operations topic: takeoff, aircraft control, positioning, altitude control, airspeed control, sensor zoom, pan, and tilt, approach, and landing [1]. Its five defined flight paths, Position, Traverse, Orbit, Spiral, and Recon, cover every combination of aircraft orientation and camera pointing an inspection or mapping pilot uses [2]. Level 2 trials add camera operation under time pressure, which pulls in the exposure control covered in [Camera Exposure](../topics/camera-exposure.md). Because the apparatus is the same one used in the AUVSI Trusted Operator flight assessment [3], time on the lane is also time toward an industry check-ride, as described in [AUVSI Trusted Operator Program](../exams/auvsi-trusted-operator-program.md).

## Equipment and the build

The apparatus is four omni bucket stands and twenty white 2 gallon buckets. Each stand is an A-frame built from 20 inch lengths of two-by-four cut at 45 degrees and fastened to a short four-by-four center post, with buckets mounted on the top and sides facing different directions; printed sticker targets go inside the buckets [4]. The whole set can be built in a few hours with a miter saw and basic carpentry skills, and a documented build came in just under $250 in materials, with the buckets themselves the largest line item [4]. That matches the program's equipment plan, which allocates about $250 in hardware-store materials for the lane. Level 2 trials want alternating white and black buckets to force exposure control [2], so buy or paint half the buckets black if the session will run Level 2 trials. Beyond the stands, the session needs a training aircraft with a zoom-capable or croppable camera per student pair, a 100 foot tape or rope for layout, landing pads for the launch point, and the NIST forms book or a simple score sheet per trial [5].

## Setup

Lay the stands in a straight line using the tape or rope, with 3 meter or 6 meter spacing depending on available space and aircraft size; the standard lane fits inside roughly 100 feet and scales from a basketball court to an open field [2] [4]. Place the launch and land platform at one end. Orient the bucket openings per the fabrication guide so that each target requires a distinct aircraft position and orientation to view [4]. Setup and teardown with hinged stands takes minutes, which matters when lab time is three hours and flying time is the point. Site selection and airspace checks follow the normal field session process in [Preflight Inspection and Go/No-Go Briefing](preflight-and-go-no-go-briefing.md).

## Running a session

Run the lane as stations. One student flies a timed trial while a second acts as timer and scorekeeper and the rest fly skill drills from [Precision Maneuvering Drills](precision-maneuvering-drills.md) in a separate area, rotating through. A full trial contains 20 targets, each with five increasingly small features to identify, for up to 100 points, and NIST typically sets the trial limit around 20 minutes so a trial fits one battery [2]. Early in the term, drop the clock and fly single paths: Position first, since hovering at defined altitudes along the centerline is the substrate skill, then Traverse and Orbit, then Spiral and Recon. Mid-term, fly full Level 1 trials, all white buckets and no zoom, which isolates positioning from camera work [2]. Late in the term, fly Level 2 trials with alternating bucket colors and maximum zoom captures, which is the exam configuration [2]. Every trial gets scored and logged, because the accumulating score history is the evidence of progress that makes a standard test method worth the lumber.

## Variations and progressions

The lane supports difficulty control without new hardware. Increase stand spacing to demand more transit control, add wind by scheduling afternoon sessions once students are ready for it, or require nose-in flying on specific paths to attack orientation dependence. For students who plateau, single-path repeats with a coach watching stick inputs find the specific habit that costs points. For students who master the lane early, the reaction course in [Dynamic Reaction Course](dynamic-reaction-course.md) removes the choreography, and the check-ride framing described in [Flight Proficiency and Practice Exercises](../topics/flight-proficiency-and-practice-exercises.md) wraps the trial in preflight risk assessment and documentation review for a full professional evaluation rehearsal.

## Assessment

The apparatus scores itself: features legibly captured convert directly to points, so grading is counting, not judgment [2]. A practical scheme grades participation and logged trials through the term, sets a midterm gate at a Level 1 trial score threshold, and uses the Level 2 trial in 25 minutes as the pass line for the course's flight outcome, exactly as the approved outline specifies [1]. Publishing a class leaderboard is optional but effective; the tests were designed to make pilot proficiency comparable, and students respond to seeing the number move.

# SMS Hazard Review and Change Management Lab

This proposed AERO 100 activity teaches the complete cycle of describing a system, identifying hazards, selecting controls, and checking results. It can also use mapping and inspection scenarios in AERO 110 and 120. Allow approximately 60 to 75 minutes for the first session and 15 minutes of a later lab for the assurance review. The method adapts [Part 5](../sources/14-cfr-part-5.md), §§ 5.51 through 5.75, and [AC 120-92D](../sources/ac-120-92d.md), Appendix B. It is not a regulatory compliance evaluation.

## Setup

Give teams a mission brief, a site sketch, the relevant aircraft instructions, a sample configuration record, and one fictional discrepancy or change request. A whiteboard or ordinary document is sufficient. Teams rotate through pilot, observer, recorder, and reviewer roles. The instructor makes the actual classroom safety decisions.

Run the initial scenario as a tabletop, simulator, or unpowered inspection exercise. Do not introduce real battery damage, radio jamming, deliberate airborne link loss, unsafe propeller installation, or live emergency conditions. If a later flight is used to gather evidence, it follows the normal approved lab procedure on an aircraft already released for that activity.

## Scenario

A team plans a short mapping practice flight from a designated launch area. A firmware update has occurred since the last lab. The configuration record does not establish whether the return-to-home settings were preserved. A newly placed obstacle is shown on the site sketch near the expected return path. The team has enough information to identify the concern, but not enough to assume a safe configuration.

The correct response need not be to fly. The team should first reconcile the configuration and site information. Appropriate changes could include postponing the flight, relocating within an authorized and suitable area, or revising and verifying the mission procedure. The instructor should challenge a proposal that merely increases return altitude without checking airspace limits, other obstacles, aircraft behavior, and the consequences of the change.

## First session

Begin with a system description. Students identify the mission, aircraft and software state, site, people, equipment, and external interfaces. They state what changed and which information is missing. They then describe hazards as conditions, possible consequences as outcomes, and risk as a severity and likelihood judgment with an explanation.

Next, students propose controls and evaluate the expected residual risk before using them. They identify who would carry out each action, who would authorize the activity, what evidence is needed, and what conditions require stopping. Students must consider whether their proposed control introduces another hazard. The instructor checks the reasoning and the proposed verification method.

Finally, teams prepare a short simulated notification to the party that could address the hazard, such as the maintainer or site coordinator. Another team reviews the record for missing assumptions. The original team revises its decision and briefs what changed and why. No external messages are sent as part of this classroom exercise.

## Record template

Use the following fields in a single document. This is an original classroom template, not an FAA form.

| Field | Student entry |
| --- | --- |
| Record ID, date, team, and operation | Identify the scenario and link the mission or equipment record. |
| Trigger and system description | State what changed, the operating context, and relevant interfaces. |
| Hazard and possible consequences | Separate the condition from the harm it could cause. |
| Initial risk and uncertainty | Explain severity, likelihood, exposure, and missing information. |
| Proposed controls | Name each action and its owner. |
| Predicted residual risk | Explain why the controls would work and whether they add hazards. |
| Decision and authority | Record proceed, revise, or no-go, with instructor review and stop conditions. |
| Verification before use | Specify the configuration check, simulation, inspection, or other suitable evidence. |
| Communication | Identify who needs to know and what the briefing or notification contains. |
| Assurance review | Record the follow-up evidence, date, reviewer, and whether to close or reopen. |

## Follow-up session

Provide evidence after the proposed change. For example, the configuration has been verified, but a later simulated flight log shows that the crew used an older checklist. Students decide whether the control was implemented as intended and whether it can be considered effective. They reopen the record when necessary, revise the procedure or training, and set another verification point. This is the safety assurance step, not just a second preflight assessment.

Compare results across teams. Ask which evidence would justify closure and which uncertainties remain. A record can remain open even when the immediate flight has been cancelled. The purpose is to resolve the underlying problem before a future crew encounters it.

## Assessment and progression

Assess whether students described the system accurately, distinguished hazard from consequence, justified their risk decisions, selected workable controls, identified authority and communication needs, and used evidence to make the closure decision. Require revision if any of those elements is missing. Do not reward a team merely for reaching a go decision or filling every box.

Repeat with a student build component substitution, a mapping site access change, or an inspection client's request for a different viewpoint. The activity should become shorter as students gain fluency. Its durable output is a defensible decision and a traceable follow-up, linked to [aeronautical decision-making](../topics/aeronautical-decision-making.md), [maintenance](../topics/maintenance-and-inspection.md), and the broader [SMS topic](../topics/safety-management-systems.md).

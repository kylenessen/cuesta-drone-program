# Dynamic Reaction Course

**Dynamic Reaction Course** is a flight lab that removes the choreography from drone training. Every other exercise in the program, the drills, the NIST lane, even the capstone trial, is a known sequence the student rehearses until smooth. This course inverts that: programmable light pods scattered across the flight area activate in an order the pilot cannot predict, and the pilot must find the lit target, fly to it, stabilize, and capture it before the next one fires. The skill trained is in-flight replanning under time pressure, which is what search, incident response, and any radio call of "actually, go look at that instead" demand of a working pilot. The idea originated in program planning as a roughly $500 target-and-obstacle build, and this page turns it into a concrete design.

## What it teaches

The course exercises the same control skills as [Precision Maneuvering Drills](precision-maneuvering-drills.md), positioning, altitude control, airspeed control, approach, all named in the approved outline [1], but forces them to happen while the pilot is deciding rather than executing a plan. It also trains scanning discipline, since the pilot or a visual observer must locate the next lit pod while the aircraft is moving, and crew communication when run in pairs, which touches the outline's human factors topic and the crew resource management thread in [Aeronautical Decision-Making](../topics/aeronautical-decision-making.md). Under a clock it produces exactly the pressure that reveals whether checklist habits and orientation control are actually automatic.

## Equipment

The clean commercial answer is a sports reaction-training system. A six-pod kit with a charging base, carrying case, and a phone app that runs randomized activation sequences lists at $439 and routinely sells in the $350 range [2]. The pods are rugged touch-sensitive RGB pucks built to be slapped by sprinting athletes, they mount flat on any surface, and the app's built-in random and sequence modes do the activation work with no programming. That price leaves $60 to $150 of the roughly $500 materials budget for mounting hardware: straps or hook-and-loop tape to fix pods onto the NIST lane's bucket stands [3], a few 5 gallon buckets or cones as freestanding mounts, and stakes for uneven ground. A do-it-yourself alternative, remote-controlled battery LED puck lights zip-tied to cones with the instructor mashing remotes as the randomizer, costs under $100 and works, but the commercial pods add automatic sequencing, timing capture, and weather resistance that repay the difference across semesters. Beyond the pods, the course needs one training aircraft, a landing pad launch point, and a stopwatch or the app's own timing.

## Setup

Scatter five or six pods across an area roughly the size of the NIST lane, about 100 feet on the long axis, at varied heights: two or three on bucket stands or fence posts near eye level, the rest at ground level on pads or buckets. Vary the facing so some targets require the camera to point down and others sideways, which imports the omni bucket logic from the lane [3]. Keep all pods inside a briefed boundary and below a briefed ceiling, and walk the course with students before the first flight so the pod positions are known even though the activation order is not. Position the pilot and instructor where the whole course is visible; the pilot flies from one fixed station, within visual line of sight, exactly as any other lab flight.

## Running the course

Brief a run as: launch, hover at the start pad, then follow the lights. The app activates one pod at a time in random order; the pilot flies to the lit pod, stabilizes in a hover close enough to fill the frame, and captures an image or holds a two-second stable frame on it, at which point the instructor or observer calls it and the next pod fires. A run of ten activations takes three to six minutes depending on skill. Score each run by total time and by capture quality, one point per target legibly captured, which mirrors the lane's capture-based scoring [3]. Run students in pairs, pilot and spotter, and swap roles between runs; the spotter calling out the next lit pod while the pilot flies is deliberate crew resource management practice, and doing a run solo afterward shows students how much the second set of eyes was worth.

## Variations and progressions

The course scales in difficulty without moving hardware. Start with generous framing standards and no clock, then tighten to timed runs. Require nose-in approaches to specific pods, or a fixed heading for the whole run, to attack orientation dependence. Use the app's color logic for decision loading: red pods must be captured from above, blue from the side, and a pod that changes color mid-approach forces a replan. For an advanced session, run two aircraft on the same course at separated altitudes with the pods split by color between them, which adds the shared-airspace awareness of [Field Operations Best Practices](../topics/field-operations-best-practices.md) under controlled conditions. The same pods also freshen other labs: mounted inside lane buckets they turn a practice trial into a randomized-order trial, and a single pod on the landing pad makes a fine finish-line for the drill station's landing ladder.

## Assessment

Score runs like lane trials: time plus captures, logged per student across the term. The number that matters is the trend, since the course is about converting deliberate control into reflexive control, and a falling run time with stable capture quality is direct evidence of that conversion. As a grading element it works best as a completion-plus-improvement grade rather than an absolute standard, because randomized orders make individual runs uneven; over five or more logged runs the noise averages out. The course is also a natural exhibition event for program visitors, which is worth something to a new program even if it never appears in a gradebook.

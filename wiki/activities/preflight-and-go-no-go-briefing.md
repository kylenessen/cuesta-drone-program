# Preflight Inspection and Go/No-Go Briefing

**Preflight Inspection and Go/No-Go Briefing** is the ground exercise that opens every AERO 100 flight lab and, run formally a few times per term, stands alone as a graded activity. The student assembles a real weather briefing for the actual site and hour, checks airspace and authorizations, inspects an actual aircraft, and delivers a spoken go or no-go decision with reasons. It is the course's most direct rehearsal of professional practice, because section 107.49 makes exactly this assessment a legal precondition of every commercial flight [2], and the approved outline builds two of its four assignments on it: preflight calculations to determine whether flight can be safely conducted, and a technical preflight inspection with an endurance estimate [1].

## What it teaches

The activity binds together material that otherwise lives in separate units: METAR and TAF decoding from [Aviation Weather Sources](../topics/aviation-weather-sources.md), the LAANC and TFR workflow from [Airspace Authorization](../topics/airspace-authorization.md), the inspection standard from [Maintenance and Inspection](../topics/maintenance-and-inspection.md), battery inspection from [Battery Fundamentals and Safety](../topics/battery-fundamentals-and-safety.md), the IMSAFE self-check from [Remote Pilot Physiology](../topics/remote-pilot-physiology.md), and the checklist discipline of [Field Operations Best Practices](../topics/field-operations-best-practices.md). The framing students should hear is that the decision is the deliverable. A no-go, argued from the data, scores as well as a go, and the hazardous attitudes covered in [Aeronautical Decision-Making](../topics/aeronautical-decision-making.md) are mostly attitudes about exactly this moment.

## Equipment and materials

The activity needs a one-page briefing form, a training aircraft with batteries, and internet access for the briefing sources: aviationweather.gov for METARs and TAFs, tfr.faa.gov for temporary flight restrictions, and a LAANC app for facility map grids and authorization status, the channels AC 107-2A points remote pilots toward [3]. The form is the durable artifact worth designing carefully. A workable layout has four blocks: weather (station used, current METAR decoded, TAF for the window, wind and gust against the aircraft's limit), airspace (class at the site, grid ceiling, authorization or FRIA status, TFR and NOTAM check), aircraft (inspection items per the manufacturer checklist, battery inspection and charge, registration and firmware, estimated endurance), and crew (IMSAFE, roles, emergency plan). The NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory's public UAS procedures, which record weather on the preflight checklist and reevaluate before each takeoff, are a good model for what the filled form should look like in practice [4].

## Setup

For lab-opening use, nothing needs staging: the day's site and aircraft are the exercise. For the formal graded version, the instructor adds controlled variation. Assign each student or pair a different briefing target: today's site now, today's site at 6 pm, a site under the San Luis Obispo airport's Class D surface area, a site in Class G up the coast. Seed some aircraft with discoverable faults, a cracked propeller from the spares bin, a battery below charge, a missing registration label or an SD card pulled from the slot, so the inspection has something real to find. The towered-airport scenario satisfies the outline's assignment to determine whether flight can be safely conducted near an airport with an operating control tower [1].

## Running the activity

Give students twenty to thirty minutes to work the form, alone or in pairs, then take briefings orally, two to three minutes each: conditions, airspace, aircraft, decision. Hold the standard that every claim traces to a source or an observation, "winds 8 gusting 14 from the METAR, aircraft limit 27, margin comfortable" rather than "weather looks fine." Faulted aircraft must be caught and dispositioned: flag it, ground it, or fix and reinspect. Where the decision is marginal, ask for the personal minimum that would flip it, which converts an argument about this flight into a policy the student carries forward. When the activity opens a real flight lab, the class flies on the strength of the accepted briefings, which is the point: the paperwork is the gate the flying actually passes through, and mature operations centralize exactly this gate in a dispatch desk, as described in [Airspace Authorization](../topics/airspace-authorization.md).

## Variations and progressions

Early in the term, run it as a guided walkthrough with the whole class briefing the same site. Mid-term, individual briefings with faults and varied scenarios. Late in the term, adversarial versions: a forecast that deteriorates mid-window, an authorization that covers the wrong polygon, a TFR that appeared overnight, or a classmate as the "client" applying schedule pressure to a marginal call, which stages the external-pressure hazardous attitude directly. The capstone version wraps the NIST Level 2 trial: the student briefs the site and aircraft, presents documentation, flies the scored lane, and hands over the completed paper trail, which is the check-ride framing the AUVSI Trusted Operator assessment uses on the same apparatus and that [Flight Proficiency and Practice Exercises](../topics/flight-proficiency-and-practice-exercises.md) recommends borrowing.

## Assessment

Grade the form and the oral briefing against a simple rubric: completeness of sources checked, correctness of decoding and airspace determination, faults found versus seeded, quality of the endurance estimate, and defensibility of the decision. The exercise repeats naturally, so early attempts can be low stakes and the graded versions late in the term can carry weight. A useful final standard: given a site and a time, the student produces a briefing an examiner could act on in under thirty minutes, catches every seeded fault, and states a decision with numbers attached. That is close to what employers' flight programs and third party assessors actually check before anyone flies.

# Sectional Chart Scavenger Hunt

**Sectional Chart Scavenger Hunt** is a classroom lab for the airspace unit that turns chart reading from a lecture topic into a timed search. Students work in small teams over the current sectional chart covering San Luis Obispo County, racing to find, decode, and defend a list of chart features. Chart reading is the tested skill behind nearly every airspace question on the Part 107 knowledge exam, and the approved outline requires aeronautical charts, chart reading, geographical coordinates, airspace, and special use airspace as course content [1]. The hunt covers all of it in one session, and it is the rare knowledge-test drill that a class treats as a game.

## What it teaches

Every hunt item is a knowledge-test skill in disguise: identifying airspace classes from boundary symbology, reading ceilings and floors from the figures inside them, decoding airport data blocks, locating a position from latitude and longitude, recognizing special use airspace and reading its panel information, and extracting obstacle heights in MSL and AGL. The deeper lesson is fluency, meaning the difference between a student who can eventually decode a chart element with the legend open and one who reads the chart the way the timed knowledge test and a preflight airspace check both require. The subject matter itself lives in [Sectional Chart](../topics/sectional-chart.md), [Airspace Classification](../topics/airspace-classification.md), and [Airport Operations](../topics/airport-operations.md); the hunt is the fluency drill on top.

## Equipment and materials

Paper charts make the best table game: one current sectional covering the local area per team of two or three, available for a few dollars each, plus the FAA Chart Users' Guide as the authoritative legend reference [2]. The FAA also publishes the same charts free as digital downloads, and a projected chart or a laptop per team running a viewer works when paper is short [3]. Each team needs the hunt sheet, a plotter or ruler helps for the coordinate items, and the classroom needs a way to show answers afterward, ideally the projected chart zoomed to each item. The only preparation cost is the instructor's hour building the hunt sheet against the current chart edition, which must be repeated when the chart cycle updates, since answers move.

## Setup

Write the hunt as 15 to 25 items in three tiers. Find items name a feature and ask for its location: the Class D airspace nearest the campus, a charted glider symbol, a group obstacle over 1,000 feet AGL. Decode items point at a location and ask what the chart says: the ceiling of the surface Class D at San Luis Obispo, the CTAF at a named non-towered airport, the floor of the Class E shelf over a named town, the meaning of the magenta vignette. Defend items ask for an operational decision: can a Part 107 flight at 400 feet AGL launch from this point without an authorization, and what on the chart says so. Include at least one latitude-longitude item that forces plotting, one military training route or MOA panel read, and one item inside the restricted airspace along the coast, which makes the local Vandenberg-area chart clutter a feature of the lesson instead of a distraction. Keep an answer key with chart panel references.

## Running the session

Run it as a race with a floor: 45 to 60 minutes, teams work the sheet, and the instructor circulates but answers only questions about the rules. Score two points for a correct answer with a correct justification, one for a correct answer poorly defended, zero otherwise, which prices guessing correctly. The debrief is where the teaching happens: project each item, have the winning team on that item explain their read, and connect each to its knowledge-test form, since several hunt items can be lifted nearly verbatim from published sample questions [4]. Items most teams missed mark the topics needing another lecture pass. The defend items deserve the most debrief time because they rehearse the real workflow: chart first, then the LAANC grid check from [Airspace Authorization](../topics/airspace-authorization.md) to confirm what the chart implied.

## Variations and progressions

A second-round variant plans a mission instead of hunting features: each team gets a hypothetical job site somewhere on the chart and must report the airspace at the surface, the authorization required, the nearest airport and its frequency to monitor, and the obstacles within a mile, which converts the hunt into the site-assessment habit professionals run before every quote. A field variant hands each team the chart location of the day's actual flight site before a lab and requires the airspace portion of the [Preflight Inspection and Go/No-Go Briefing](preflight-and-go-no-go-briefing.md) to be argued from the chart alone before the apps are opened. For exam-week review, run the hunt again with a different item list and compare team scores against the first round; the improvement is visible and motivating.

## Assessment

The scored sheet grades itself, but the durable assessment is individual: a follow-up quiz that reuses the hunt's item styles on a different chart region shows whether fluency transferred or lived in one teammate. Chart items also slot directly into the course's written exams, and tracking which hunt items the class missed gives the instructor a targeted review list for the Part 107 test window. A student who can clear the defend tier unaided is ready for the airspace portion of the knowledge test, which is the outcome the unit exists to produce [1].

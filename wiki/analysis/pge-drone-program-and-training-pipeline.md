# PG&E Drone Program and Training Pipeline

**PG&E Drone Program and Training Pipeline** documents how Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) staffs, trains, and qualifies its drone pilots, how the IBEW 1245 union has trained utility workers to fly, and what precedent exists for community college credit to flow into and out of a utility training pipeline. The short answer: PG&E runs one of the largest utility drone programs in the world out of a roughly 50 person Aviation Services organization, treats the FAA Part 107 certificate as the entry credential, and layers proprietary initial, recurrent, and mission-specific training on top of it. The union's own 40 hour course assumed the same structure, taking pilots who were already certificated and teaching them utility craft. That seam, Part 107 plus general airmanship below and employer-specific qualification above, is exactly where AERO 100 fits, and California's credit for prior learning framework plus several existing utility and union partnerships give the articulation conversation working precedent.

## Question

Cuesta College is planning a utility infrastructure inspection course and has had an enthusiastic initial response from PG&E. Designing the course and the partnership requires knowing how PG&E's pipeline actually works. What does the qualification ladder above Part 107 look like, who flies, how has the IBEW 1245 training program evolved, and what would it take for AERO 100 to count toward a utility pipeline or for utility training to earn Cuesta credit? ## Method

Public sources only, gathered July 2026. The core sources are a 2025 Vertical Mag feature on PG&E Aviation Services, PG&E press materials and an EPIC regulatory report, drone industry trade coverage, IBEW 1245's own news posts, and California Community Colleges Chancellor's Office credit for prior learning policy documents. PG&E's internal training standards are not published, so the qualification ladder is reconstructed from journalism and hedged accordingly.

## Program structure

PG&E's drone operations sit inside Aviation Services, a department of roughly 50 people in a company of 28,000, directed by Chris Courtney. The department oversees all of the utility's flying, including about 30,000 flight hours per year of contracted helicopter operations alongside the drone program [1]. Within Aviation Services, Kellen Kirk manages UAS and fixed-wing operations, Jon Teach manages the dock-based remote operations program, and Bounmy Daoheuang serves as program manager for learning and development, a dedicated UAS training function inside the aviation organization [1].

Flight Core is the program's in-house communications and dispatch center, managed by Dannielle Lance. Every drone pilot flying for PG&E, employee or contractor, receives the same morning briefing that Flight Core provides to crewed helicopter pilots, covering weather, temporary flight restrictions, NOTAMs, fire index, known hazards, and aircraft deconfliction. Flight Core also tracks incidents in the field, including customer confrontations [1].

The scale is large. Roughly 80 drone pilots are in the field on a typical day, a mix of PG&E employees and contractors, and the program completed more than 300,000 pole and tower inspections by drone in 2024 [1]. The program began in 2015 with transmission inspections, around 60,000 towers per year, and scaled dramatically after the November 2018 Camp Fire pushed system inspection to the center of the company [1] [2]. Jason Regan, PG&E's vice president of system inspections, describes it as "one of the largest and most advanced drone-based equipment inspection programs in the world" [2]. Most of the inspection fleet is DJI, which matters for equipment planning because it is the same ecosystem the [NDAA Compliance and Blue UAS](../topics/ndaa-compliance-and-blue-uas.md) page notes is restricted for public agencies but remains standard in investor-owned utility work [1].

The work itself, high zoom visual and thermal inspection of energized assets under CPUC inspection mandates, is covered in [Infrastructure Inspection Operations](../topics/infrastructure-inspection-operations.md), and the hazards specific to flying near conductors in [Flying Near Energized Infrastructure](../topics/flying-near-energized-infrastructure.md).

## The qualification ladder

Every PG&E drone operator holds an FAA [Remote Pilot Certificate](../regulations/remote-pilot-certificate.md) under Part 107. That is the floor, not the qualification. On top of it PG&E mandates its own certifications: initial and recurrent web-based training modules covering technical and safety topics, plus mission-specific certifications developed internally and updated as procedures change and field lessons accumulate [1]. The company runs a safety management system with an aviation safety action program, and pushes its operation toward crewed-aviation standards such as IS-BAO [1]. Field pilots carry satellite communicators for emergency contact, and the BVLOS side of the program documents pilot participation, flight logs, maintenance, and post-maintenance functional test flights to satisfy FAA waiver conditions [1] [5].

The published record does not spell out hours, syllabi, or renewal cycles for the internal tiers. What it does establish is the shape: a proprietary employer qualification stack, administered by a dedicated learning and development function, sitting on the public Part 107 foundation. It also establishes who flies. Inspection data collection is performed by dedicated pilots, both in-house crews and heavy contractor use, rather than being distributed across the general line workforce [1]. One Aviation Services supervisor is noted as having brought in-house inspection operations over from the line department, suggesting the centralized model consolidated flying that once lived in operations [1]. The IBEW record below shows the complementary model, linemen and other field classifications using drones as a tool of their trade.

## Remote and automated operations

PG&E became the first California utility to fly fully remote drone operations in 2023, under an FAA beyond visual line of sight waiver developed with Skydio [2] [3]. As of 2025 seven Skydio docks, combined hangar and charging stations, are deployed from Redding south past Paso Robles and into the Sierra Nevada, flown from PG&E's aviation facility in Concord over LTE or Starlink connectivity. The docked aircraft, upgraded from the Skydio X2 to the X10, inspect substations and hydroelectric penstocks and aim at rapid fault response, and the waiver's permissions have expanded progressively [1]. The dock demonstration ran as EPIC project 3.41, a ratepayer-funded research effort authorized in April 2022 and concluded in May 2025 [4]. The company also flies gas pipeline leak surveys and in 2023 became the first North American utility to string powerline with drones, using Infravision's tensioned stringing system for storm repairs and reconductoring [2] [3]. The pending [Part 108 BVLOS Rulemaking](../regulations/part-108-bvlos-rulemaking.md) would normalize this category of operation beyond individual waivers.

Remote dock operations matter for curriculum because they represent the direction of utility staffing: fewer pilots standing under the asset, more remote operators, mission planners, and data reviewers working from an operations center.

## The IBEW 1245 training model

IBEW Local 1245, which represents PG&E's field workforce, pushed for years to bring drone work in-house rather than leave it with non-signatory contractors, framing drone flying as work that belongs to its members [6]. In October 2019 the union and PG&E launched a first of its kind in-house drone operator training. The beta course ran 40 hours at PG&E's Gas Safety Academy in Winters, included 15 hours of flight time, and put a dozen IBEW 1245 members through four tests by the end of the week. Trainees arrived already holding Part 107 certificates but lacking hands-on experience and utility protocol. The curriculum covered precision site photography down to features an inch across, standard and thermal cameras, drone software, PG&E standard operating procedures, airspace, and weather. Instruction came through PrecisionHawk, with UAS program manager Robert Henley overseeing the session, and a pilot program was planned through March 2020 to inform a permanent program [6].

A second documented round ran in fall 2021 at the Woodland JATC facility, and its shape had shifted. The twenty trainees were IBEW 1245 members employed by other utilities, including Liberty Utilities and NV Energy, and the course now combined drone license preparation with utility-specific hands-on training for linemen, rather than assuming certificated entrants [7]. The union's framing is drones as a tool of the trade: linemen and troublemen patrolling lines, finding outage causes, examining equipment, and locating access points in remote terrain without climbing structures or calling helicopters [7].

No IBEW 1245 publication about the drone program after December 2021 was found in this pass, so whether the course became a standing program, and what it looks like now, is not established by public sources. The two documented sessions still carry the durable lesson: the union course is a Part 107 plus utility craft package of roughly one work week, and its content overlaps heavily with what a college inspection course would teach.

## Articulation precedent

California community colleges have a general legal mechanism for both directions of articulation. Title 5 section 55050 authorizes credit for prior learning, letting faculty award course credit for validated learning acquired outside the classroom through credit by examination, evaluation of industry-recognized certifications, military transcript evaluation, standardized exams, and portfolio review. Colleges may award the credit as course, general education, or elective credit, with course credit preferred to protect transfer, and discipline faculty validate the equivalence [8] [9]. The Chancellor's Office reissued implementation guidelines in February 2024 and runs a statewide demonstration project through 2030 aiming to reach 250,000 Californians, most of them working adults and apprentices [8] [9] [10].

The apprenticeship precedent is direct. California community colleges already deliver the classroom side of state-registered apprenticeships as credit coursework, and completion can satisfy degree requirements. Santiago Canyon College, for example, hosts apprenticeships including electrician and power lineman, and completing its carpentry framing apprenticeship satisfies the major requirements of an associate of science degree [11]. The state's Mapping Articulated Pathways initiative, the platform behind CPL implementation, directed 1.4 million dollars in January 2026 specifically at mapping construction trades and IBEW electrician apprenticeship competencies to course outcomes at Santiago Canyon College and Norco College, with intent to scale statewide [10]. Nationally, the electrical training ALLIANCE's arrangement with the Community College of Allegheny County counts a completed IBEW inside-wireman apprenticeship as up to 40 credits toward an associate of science in electrical construction technology, leaving only general education to finish [12]. Union training earning college credit is therefore an established pattern, not a novel ask.

The utility-to-college precedent runs through PG&E itself. PowerPathway, PG&E's workforce development program, has partnered with community colleges, training centers, and workforce agencies since 2008 to prepare candidates for utility craft hiring, with over 1,300 graduates and roughly 86 percent hired by PG&E or the utility industry [13]. Its signature programs run 160 to 480 hours led by PG&E instructors, and program literature also describes affinity programs in which PG&E advises an existing community college curriculum rather than teaching it [13] [14]. PowerPathway is a hiring pathway, not a credit arrangement, and no UAS-specific PowerPathway program was found, but it demonstrates that PG&E already co-designs community college curriculum against its hiring needs.

## What this means for the course

The pipeline has a natural seam, and AERO 100 sits on the lower side of it. Both PG&E's internal ladder and the 2019 union course treat Part 107 plus basic airmanship as the entry layer that someone else provides, then add utility craft on top. A Cuesta sequence that produces certificated pilots with real flight hours, precision camera work, thermal fundamentals, and energized infrastructure awareness delivers precisely the layer the 2019 cohort was missing when it arrived, and overlaps much of the 40 hour course's own syllabus. The realistic form of "AERO 100 counts toward the pipeline" is therefore recognition rather than credit: PG&E or a training partner accepting the course as satisfying the entry layer or shortening employer training, on the PowerPathway affinity model of a utility endorsing college curriculum. PG&E's internal certifications are proprietary safety qualifications and are unlikely to be delegated outright.

The reverse direction, utility or union training earning Cuesta credit, has ready mechanisms today. Section 55050 lets Cuesta faculty evaluate a completed utility training program or industry certification and award course credit, credit by exam covers cases without formal certificates, and the MAP work with IBEW electrician apprentices shows state policy actively funding exactly this kind of mapping. A worker who completed a utility drone course could plausibly receive credit for AERO 100 or part of the inspection course, which makes the degree pathway attractive to incumbent workers rather than only to new students.

One framing consideration for partnership design rather than curriculum: drone flying at PG&E lives in two worlds, a centralized Aviation Services organization with dedicated pilots and contractors, and a represented field workforce whose union has trained members to fly and has publicly claimed drone work as members' work. Course and partnership design should stay neutral about how that division evolves and should be legible to both audiences.

## Caveats

The PG&E sections rest on journalism and company press material, not on internal training standards, so the qualification ladder is a reconstruction of shape rather than a syllabus. Reported pilot counts vary between tellings of the same program, from 75 to 80 daily. The 2019 and 2021 IBEW articles differ on Robert Henley's affiliation, and the union record simply stops after 2021, which may reflect the union's publishing habits rather than the program's fate. The MAP apprenticeship pilot was funded in January 2026 with completion targeted for June 2026, and its outcomes were not yet published when this was written. All time-sensitive claims are as of July 2026.

# NIST Flight Proficiency Programs

AERO 100 makes a timed NIST test lane its flight capstone, treating a standard proficiency run the way a driver's license treats the road test: a repeatable, scored demonstration that the student can actually fly the aircraft they built. That design is not invented here. A small ecosystem of agencies, credentialing bodies, universities, and community colleges already operationalizes the NIST Standard Test Methods for Small UAS as a graded flying credential, and their choices are worth mining before Cuesta finalizes its own. This page surveys those existing programs and pulls out what each implies for the capstone. The apparatus and scoring themselves are documented in [Flight Proficiency and Practice Exercises](../topics/flight-proficiency-and-practice-exercises.md); this page is about who runs the tests as a credential and what they have learned doing it.

## The shared measurement standard

Every program below rests on the same foundation, the Standard Test Methods for Small Unmanned Aircraft Systems developed at NIST's Intelligent Systems Division and standardized through ASTM International's E54.09 subcommittee on response robots, with sponsorship from the Department of Homeland Security Science and Technology Directorate [1] [2]. The methods were built for public safety, so that an agency could know before an incident whether a given pilot and aircraft could perform, and the properties that made them useful there, cheap apparatus, embedded scoring, and repeatability, are exactly what make them portable into a classroom [2]. Two artifacts recur across the programs. The first is the family of [Open Test Lanes](../exams/nist-open-test-lane.md) at Levels 1 through 5, from basic maneuvering up through confined spaces. The second is the [Basic Proficiency Evaluation for Remote Pilots](../exams/basic-proficiency-evaluation-for-remote-pilots.md), or BPERP, a compact three-stand entry test that most credentialing programs use as their gateway. The BPERP can be administered in about ten minutes using three standard omni bucket stands, a fifty foot tape measure, and a stopwatch, which is why it, rather than a full open lane, is what most credential holders actually fly [3] [5].

## The public safety credential: APSA BPERP

The most established credential built on the standard is the Airborne Public Safety Association's Basic Proficiency Evaluation for Remote Pilots certificate. APSA is a long running public safety aviation nonprofit, and its BPERP certificate is awarded to pilots who pass the NIST test lane under a proctor, building directly on the 2020 NIST BPERP test methods [3] [4]. The credential requires a current Part 107 certificate as its entry ticket, which positions it as a flying credential layered on top of the FAA's knowledge test rather than a replacement for it [3]. APSA delivers training and certification in cooperation with Embry-Riddle and with public safety partners such as AIRT and DroneResponders, and it offers proctor and advanced proctor courses so agencies can run the evaluation in house [4] [6]. Adoption is real within its sector. The Airborne Public Safety Association, DroneResponders, and the National Fire Protection Association have all pointed programs at this methodology, and NFPA 2400, the standard for public safety sUAS, references NIST style job performance requirements [6] [7]. For AERO 100 the lesson is structural. BPERP shows a clean way to package a NIST run as an awardable credential stacked on Part 107, which is the same shape the capstone already has.

## The proctor pipeline

Because the tests are meant to proliferate, several organizations exist mainly to certify the people who administer them. The Regional Training Center runs a three day, twenty four hour Train the Trainer course that certifies graduates as proctors across Level 1 Basic Proficiency, Level 2 Maneuvering, Level 3 Open, Level 4 Obstructed, and Level 5 Confined lanes plus NIST scenarios, so they can reproduce the tests at their own agencies [8]. DroneResponders and AIRT run comparable proctor courses aimed at public safety [4] [6]. This pipeline matters to Cuesta in a specific way. If the program wants its capstone score to carry weight beyond the transcript, the path is to have an instructor certified as a proctor through one of these courses, at which point the same lane the students train on can issue a recognized proficiency result rather than a purely internal grade. It is the flight-side analogue to the instructor accreditation question raised for the [AUVSI Trusted Operator Program](../exams/auvsi-trusted-operator-program.md), whose upper level Practical Flight Assessment is itself delivered on NIST built apparatus [9].

## The academic adopters

Two academic precedents matter most because they are the closest to Cuesta's situation. Colorado State University's Drone Center offers a NIST UAV Operator Certification covering Levels 1, 2, and 3 of the Open Lane tests, administered several days a week to any interested pilot [10]. It is instructive as a model of a university running the lanes as a standalone, scheduled, for-fee skills credential, though CSU presents it as a skills validation offering rather than as something wired into a degree, and Level 3 there demands a camera with strong zoom, at least twenty eight times, which sets an equipment floor worth noting if the capstone ever climbs to Level 3 [10].

The more direct precedent is Carroll Community College in Maryland, whose commercial drone pilot certificate explicitly prepares students for two credentials, the FAA Part 107 certificate and the BPERP certificate, and has students fly the NIST remote pilot test lane to demonstrate control and accuracy, across at least six Saturdays of hands on flight training blended with classroom and simulator work [11]. Carroll is essentially the model Cuesta is reaching for, a community college that pairs the FAA knowledge credential with a NIST flying credential inside one course, and it is worth a direct look and possibly a conversation as the AERO 100 design firms up.

## The data behind the benchmark

The most useful single source for setting the capstone's numbers is a 2023 study by Colin Dees and Joseph Burgett at Clemson University, published in the Micro Nano Technology Education Center's journal, which set out to give two-year institutions a data-driven proficiency benchmark rather than an arbitrary cutoff [12]. Working from BPERP results, the authors built percentile-based flight-time tiers, placing the top ten percent of pilots at or under five minutes and nine seconds and the median around seven to eight minutes, and they validated that a simplified BPERP using only the first two maneuvers predicts overall performance about as well as the full five-maneuver suite, which lets a resource-limited college test efficiently [12]. Their headline finding is the one that should shape the AERO 100 rubric. Ninety three percent of participants passed the image scoring portion of the test, but only forty eight percent met the time requirement, meaning time, not image quality, is where a NIST test actually separates pilots [12]. Two cautions apply when carrying these numbers over. The Clemson figures describe the short BPERP, not the Level 2 Open Test Lane the current AERO 100 outline names, so the specific five minute nine second mark is a BPERP benchmark and cannot be transplanted onto the Level 2 lane's twenty five minute allowance without its own data. What does transfer is the structural insight, that the binding constraint is the clock, which argues for treating the capstone's time limit as the real difficulty knob and for building the drill progression around working faster rather than merely cleaner.

## Simulation as a practice surface

One adjacent development is worth tracking for practice rather than assessment. The Zephyr drone simulator has integrated a NIST payload delivery test method, letting pilots rehearse a standardized scored task in software before flying it live [13]. For a semester long build course where flight time competes with build time and weather, a simulator that scores against the same methodology is a plausible way to move early proficiency work off the physical lane and protect student-built aircraft from the roughest early attempts.

## What this means for AERO 100

The landscape confirms the capstone is on solid ground and sharpens three decisions. First, the credential shape is proven: BPERP stacked on Part 107, as APSA awards it and Carroll Community College teaches it, is the established way to package a NIST run as an awardable flying credential, and it is the natural target if Cuesta wants the capstone to mean something outside the transcript. Second, recognition runs through proctoring: to make the score portable, the move is to certify an instructor as a NIST proctor via the Regional Training Center or a DroneResponders course, not to invent a local standard. Third, the rubric should be built around time. The Clemson data shows nearly everyone can capture the imagery, so the time limit is what makes the test a test, which means the training weeks and the go, no-go threshold should be tuned to the clock. The nearest thing to a decision still open is whether AERO 100 should align its capstone to the short BPERP that the credentialing world actually uses, rather than the fuller Level 2 Open Lane the current outline names, since matching BPERP would let student results plug directly into an existing certificate and into the Clemson benchmark data. That tradeoff belongs in the capstone design conversation and is flagged here rather than resolved. A separate and prior question, what the capstone should reward at all, timed speed or careful control, is treated in [Flight Proficiency Assessment Models](flight-proficiency-assessment-models.md).

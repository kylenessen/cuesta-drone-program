# Applying Safety Management to the Drone Curriculum

The recommendation from Kyle's September 22, 2026 conversation with an aviation safety professional is to use Part 5 as the organizing framework for safety instruction. The proposed application is to run a small, documented safety process across the semester. Students should leave knowing how a professional operation learns from hazards and changes its procedures. This proposal does not amend the authoritative course outlines or establish college policy.

## What to teach first

Start with the [Part 5 applicability distinction](../regulations/part-5-safety-management-systems.md). Part 107 governs the typical small-drone teaching flight assumed here. Part 5 provides an organizational safety model that the program can voluntarily use. Neither an SMS worksheet nor an instructor's approval substitutes for the remote PIC's responsibilities and applicable operating rules.

Assign [Part 5](../sources/14-cfr-part-5.md), §§ 5.1, 5.3, and 5.5, followed by the operational sections in Subparts B through F. Pair those with [AC 120-92D](../sources/ac-120-92d.md), Chapter 2 and Appendix G. Instructors should also use Appendix B for worksheet design and Appendix C for policy discussion. These are manageable excerpts with a direct classroom purpose.

Use [Annex 19](../sources/icao-annex-19.md) to explain the international background, with the edition transition clearly dated. Use [Doc 9859](../sources/icao-doc-9859.md) as deeper instructor reading. The FAA text is already available locally. The ICAO records provide access and reading guidance, not full-text conversions.

## A requirement to classroom crosswalk

The classroom activities and evidence below are proposed adaptations. The section references identify their basis. They do not claim that a student assignment satisfies all requirements for an accepted SMS.

| Part 5 basis | What students should learn | Proposed evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| §§ 5.21, 5.23 and 5.25 | Identify responsibility, resources, reporting expectations, and authority to accept risk | A short team safety charter with real instructor escalation |
| § 5.27 | Coordinate the crew's emergency actions with the host site's response | A tabletop briefing with responsibilities and verified contact arrangements |
| §§ 5.51 and 5.53 | Recognize when a change requires review and describe the whole operation | A system description and a change assessment |
| § 5.55 | Distinguish hazard from consequence, justify risk judgments, and evaluate controls before use | An initial and residual risk rationale with a named decision maker |
| § 5.57 | Inform another party that can address a hazard | A simulated site-owner or maintainer notification |
| §§ 5.71, 5.73 and 5.75 | Review evidence and correct ineffective controls | A finding, action record, follow-up observation, and closure decision |
| §§ 5.91 and 5.93 | Explain a safety change and demonstrate competence in the changed task | A short crew briefing and observed demonstration |
| §§ 5.95 and 5.97 | Preserve the current procedure and the evidence behind decisions | A versioned safety portfolio with linked records |

## AERO 100

Introduce the reporting process and the right to pause before the first practical session. Run a brief exercise in which students distinguish a hazard, a consequence, and a control. Then attach the [existing go/no-go briefing](../activities/preflight-and-go-no-go-briefing.md) to a shared hazard register so recurring problems remain visible across labs.

The [student-built aircraft](../equipment/aero-100-student-built-drone.md) makes change management tangible. A new frame, propeller, firmware version, or flight mode changes the system. Before first flight after a material change, students explain what changed, which hazards could follow, and how they will verify the proposed controls. The instructor retains flight-release authority within the course's adopted procedures. Students can simulate management roles without being assigned institutional authority they do not have.

Have each team complete the [hazard review lab](../activities/sms-hazard-review-and-change-management.md) before the first build flight. Revisit its record after flight evidence is available. At the capstone, ask students to explain one real safety decision and show how they checked the result. This adds professional judgment to the existing flight assessment without claiming that SMS is a separate FAA pilot credential.

## AERO 110

Apply the same process to mapping missions. Students describe the area, access paths, terrain, planned flight lines, launch and recovery sites, crew workload, and interactions with landowners or other field workers. A request for lower altitude, more coverage, or a different launch site becomes a change-review exercise. Students should be able to decline a mission change when the operating conditions cannot be made acceptable.

Distinguish product quality from aviation safety. Poor overlap may produce a weak map. Repeated reflying under battery or daylight pressure can create a safety problem. Ask students to document where those concerns interact. Reuse [mapping mission planning](../topics/mapping-mission-planning.md) and [field operations](../topics/field-operations-best-practices.md) as the operational context.

## AERO 120

Inspection work adds interfaces with asset owners, maintenance personnel, work crews, and site emergency arrangements. Use a tabletop scenario in which a client changes the work area or requests closer imagery. Students identify the new hazards, decide who can control them, and prepare the relevant notification. Utility inspection examples should exercise coordination and judgment without prescribing unverified separation distances or treating an SMS review as permission to approach energized equipment.

The [infrastructure inspection](../topics/infrastructure-inspection-operations.md) and [energized infrastructure](../topics/flying-near-energized-infrastructure.md) pages supply the application context. Crew workload, degraded positioning, loss of link, and pressure to obtain a better image are useful scenario prompts, subject to aircraft and site-specific verification.

## Keep the paperwork useful

Start with a short safety charter, a hazard and action register, a change-review record, and a brief periodic review. Use one record identifier to connect a report, the decision, the procedure revision, the briefing, and the later evidence. Each record should answer what changed, why, who is responsible, and how the result will be checked. A separate form for every flight is unnecessary if an existing briefing and flight log already capture the needed evidence.

As a suggested pilot, reserve ten minutes of the weekly lab for reviewing open actions and one short review at midterm and at the end of the term. These are scheduling suggestions, not FAA requirements. Grade the quality of reasoning, the suitability of controls, and the completeness of follow-up. A justified no-go decision can demonstrate success. Accident-free flying by itself cannot demonstrate that the process works.

## Decisions before institutional adoption

The program needs to identify the college leader who can authorize resources and accept organizational risk, agree on the instructor's escalation path, and coordinate with campus safety and host sites. It also needs actual reporting access, confidentiality limits, record retention, emergency arrangements, and a method for approving changes to the aircraft or operating procedures. Those decisions require college ownership. They should not be implied by publishing a wiki page.

Formal FAA recognition can be considered later if it serves a program objective. The immediate teaching value comes from a repeatable process and visible follow-through. Before using the international reference in a class after November 26, 2026, recheck Annex 19's applicable edition. Before offering operational compliance advice, recheck the current FAA rule and the authority under which the flight will occur.

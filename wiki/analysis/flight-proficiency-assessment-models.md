# Flight Proficiency Assessment Models

AERO 100's flight capstone, the practical "driver's test" a student passes on the drone they built, is settled in principle but undecided in what it should reward. The [NIST Open Test Lane](../exams/nist-open-test-lane.md) named in the current outline is a timed, self-scoring test, and its own benchmark data shows that time is what it actually measures. A program that wants to reward careful, competent control rather than speed or athleticism has to decide whether to reconfigure that test or adopt a different assessment model altogether. This page lays out the models so the choice can be made deliberately. It documents three families of flight assessment, timed self-scoring, the competence-based checkride, and tolerance-based scoring, with the existing practical drone exams in Canada and the United Kingdom as worked examples of the control-focused approach.

The decision is not resolved here. The stated program preference is for good control over quickness, which points away from a stopwatch and toward the competence and tolerance models below, but the tradeoffs belong in the capstone conversation.

## Why timed scoring rewards speed

The reason this decision matters is specific, not philosophical. On a timed NIST lane the target imagery is not hard to capture given enough time. The Clemson benchmark study found that ninety-three percent of pilots passed the image-scoring portion but only forty-eight percent met the time requirement, which means the clock, not the flying, is doing almost all of the discriminating [1]. A capstone that simply removed the time limit from a NIST lane would therefore stop separating pilots at all, because nearly everyone passes the capture task. Rewarding control instead of speed is not a matter of loosening the clock on the existing test. It requires a scoring basis built on precision, and that is what the other models supply.

## NIST scoring is the administrator's to set

A common misreading is that the NIST method dictates a pass score. It does not. NIST supplies the apparatus, the flight tasks, and the point-capture mechanism, but recommends no scoring metric and leaves the passing benchmark to whoever runs the test, so agencies set their own thresholds [2]. That matters here because it means the cheap, self-scoring bucket lane can be kept and graded on precision rather than time. Two levers exist within the NIST apparatus itself. The program can grade on capture completeness and alignment quality with a generous time ceiling set only to fit one battery, rather than making elapsed time the differentiator. It can also lean on Level 1 of the lane, which uses all-white buckets and no camera zoom and so isolates pure positioning and orientation control from camera work [3]. This is the least disruptive path, since it reuses the apparatus and the [NIST Open Lane Practice Sessions](../activities/nist-open-lane-practice.md) already designed, and only changes what earns the grade.

## The competence-based checkride

The closest existing models to what the program wants are the practical drone exams that other countries already require, which the United States does not. Both grade competent control against an examiner's judgment rather than against a clock.

Canada's RPAS flight review is the practical test for the Advanced Operations certificate, and it is explicitly competence-based rather than timed [4]. A Transport Canada-approved flight reviewer, affiliated with a flight school, asks the candidate to demonstrate a set of skills and judges whether each is performed safely and competently, with a simple pass or fail result and unlimited retakes [4]. The demonstrated skills include preflight inspection, a stable takeoff and hover, controlled flight patterns such as figure eights, orbits, and straight-line navigation, emergency procedures such as responding to a simulated motor failure, and a safe landing [5]. The exercises are specified in Appendix A of the Knowledge Requirements document, TP 15263, and the reviewer works from a published Flight Reviewer's Guide, so the criteria are open and minable for a maneuver list [6] [7]. This is a genuine "drone driver's test" whose entire basis is careful control, and it pairs a knowledge exam with a practical flight review in the same way AERO 100 pairs the [Part 107 Knowledge Test](../exams/part-107-knowledge-test.md) with its capstone.

The United Kingdom's General VLOS Certificate works similarly. The Civil Aviation Authority calls it a certificate of competency, and earning it requires a practical flight test, roughly two hours long, conducted outdoors on a drone the candidate brings, in which the pilot performs a site survey, a set of controlled manoeuvres, emergency drills, and site management to an assessor's satisfaction [8] [9]. Like the Canadian review it certifies that the pilot can operate safely and competently, and nowhere does speed enter the grade [9].

## Tolerance-based scoring

A third model borrows from manned-aviation checkrides, where maneuvers are graded against published numeric tolerances rather than a stopwatch or an examiner's overall impression. The FAA Airman Certification Standards, for instance, define a private pilot maneuver as satisfactory when the pilot holds altitude and heading within stated bands. Translated to a small drone, the same idea produces objective, precision-based tasks that ignore speed entirely: hold position inside a marked box for a set number of seconds, hold altitude within a defined band, fly a box pattern or figure eight with smooth coordinated inputs and constant heading, and land within a target circle. Because the pass criterion is a distance tolerance rather than a time, this model rewards exactly the steadiness the program is after, and it can be applied either to the checkride maneuvers above or to the NIST lane. The drill progression that builds these skills is already documented in [Precision Maneuvering Drills](../activities/precision-maneuvering-drills.md), and hobby precision exercises for hover holds, box patterns, and nose-in orientation are a further concrete source [10].

## The underlying competency standard

Beneath these models sits a common training standard worth naming. ASTM F3266, the Standard Guide for Training for Remote Pilot in Command, describes the knowledge, skills, and abilities a competent remote pilot should demonstrate, and it is the flight standard the [AUVSI Trusted Operator Program](../exams/auvsi-trusted-operator-program.md) assessment is built on [11]. It is a competency framework rather than a scored test, so it does not settle the speed-versus-control question by itself, but a control-focused rubric can be anchored to it for defensibility. The document is sold rather than published openly, which is the main barrier to using it directly.

## What is still open

The capstone can take any of four shapes, and the choice is the program's to make. A reconfigured NIST lane keeps the cheap apparatus and grades on precision with a loose time ceiling. A competence checkride modeled on the Canadian review or the UK certificate grades a named set of maneuvers against an examiner's judgment of safe control. A tolerance-based test scores those same maneuvers against numeric distance bands for full objectivity. A blend runs checkride maneuvers with tolerance scoring on the inexpensive NIST stands, combining an open apparatus, objective grading, and a control-first basis. The stated preference for competent control over speed favors the middle three or a de-timed version of the first, and the natural next step is to read the Canadian Flight Reviewer's Guide and TP 15263 Appendix A, the UK GVC assessment description, and ASTM F3266 for their maneuver lists before fixing the rubric. The credential-side counterpart to this design question, whether to align the capstone to an awardable certificate, is treated in [NIST Flight Proficiency Programs](nist-flight-proficiency-programs.md).

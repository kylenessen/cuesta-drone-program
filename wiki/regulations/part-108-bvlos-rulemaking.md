# Part 108 BVLOS Rulemaking

The **Part 108 BVLOS Rulemaking** is the FAA's in-progress effort to normalize beyond visual line of sight (BVLOS) drone operations, published as a notice of proposed rulemaking titled Normalizing Unmanned Aircraft Systems Beyond Visual Line of Sight Operations on August 7, 2025. Today, flying beyond visual line of sight requires waiving section 107.31 through a [Certificate of Waiver](/regulations/certificate-of-waiver.md); Part 108 would replace that per-operation waiver system with a standing regulatory framework. For curriculum planning it is the largest regulatory change on the horizon, though as of July 2026 it remains a proposal, and course content should continue to teach the visual line of sight rule as law. [1](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/28/2026-01644/normalizing-unmanned-aircraft-systems-beyond-visual-line-of-sight-operations-reopening-of-comment) [2](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/BVLOS_NPRM_website_version.pdf)

## Requirements

As proposed, the rule would create two levels of approval, permitted operations for lower-risk cases and an operational certificate for higher-risk ones, with risk tiers keyed to five population density categories for the operating area. Aerial surveying, agriculture, package delivery, infrastructure inspection, and civic interest operations are all named permitted-operation categories. It would approve operational areas rather than individual flights, generally below 400 feet, and it leans heavily on technology: position awareness through ADS-B reception and Remote ID, detect and avoid capability, and a companion proposal, Part 146, creating certified Automated Data Service Providers whose traffic awareness and conformance services operators can rely on. Personnel roles also change, with operations supervisors and flight coordinators managing multiple aircraft under duty and rest limits instead of one remote pilot in command per flight. The proposal's most contested element would give BVLOS drones presumptive right of way over crewed aircraft not broadcasting position electronically in certain low altitude circumstances, a departure from the categorical yield rule in the current [Part 107 Operating Rules](/regulations/part-107-operating-rules.md). These provisions describe the proposal, not current law, and any of them may change in the final rule. [2](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/BVLOS_NPRM_website_version.pdf) [3](https://dronexl.co/2026/06/18/faa-part-108-presumptive-right-of-way-bvlos-delay/) [4](https://pilotinstitute.com/part-108-explained/)

## Status

The initial comment period closed October 6, 2025 and drew more than 3,000 substantive comments. After listening sessions with manufacturers and industry associations in January 2026, the FAA reopened the comment record on January 28, 2026 for two weeks, limited to seven questions about ADS-B Out, electronic conspicuity, and detect and avoid, closing February 11, 2026. [1](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/28/2026-01644/normalizing-unmanned-aircraft-systems-beyond-visual-line-of-sight-operations-reopening-of-comment) Executive Order 14307, Unleashing American Drone Dominance, signed June 6, 2025, directed a final rule on a schedule pointing to early 2026. That deadline passed without publication, and as of the last check of this page no final rule has been released. Industry reporting attributes the delay largely to the right-of-way provision, which drew opposition from general aviation and experimental aircraft groups in more than half of all substantive comments. A reasonable read in July 2026 is a final rule in late 2026 at the earliest, but that is a projection, not an announcement. This page carries a `last_checked` date and should be reverified each term before Part 108 claims appear in course materials. [3](https://dronexl.co/2026/06/18/faa-part-108-presumptive-right-of-way-bvlos-delay/) [5](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/06/11/2025-10814/unleashing-american-drone-dominance)

The curriculum implication is timing. If a final rule lands during a course development cycle, the advanced course content on waivers and BVLOS-adjacent work (mapping large sites, linear inspections) may need revision, while the AERO 100 knowledge-test content stays anchored to Part 107 until the FAA revises the [Part 107 Knowledge Test](/exams/part-107-knowledge-test.md) and its ACS. For mapping specifically, Part 108 names aerial surveys and infrastructure inspection as permitted categories, which would move corridor mapping, large ranches, and repetitive facility inspection from waiver-by-waiver approval to standing authorization, most readily in the low density areas that describe most rural San Luis Obispo County work. See [Limits of Drone Mapping](/topics/limits-of-drone-mapping.md) for the line of sight constraint this would relax.

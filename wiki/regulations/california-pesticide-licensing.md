# California Pesticide Licensing

**California pesticide licensing** is the state layer of the drone spray operator's regulatory stack, administered by the Department of Pesticide Regulation (DPR) with enforcement delegated to county agricultural commissioners. The division of labor is worth teaching plainly: the FAA decides whether the aircraft may fly and dispense at all, through [Part 107](part-107.md) and [Part 137 Agricultural Operations](part-137-agricultural-operations.md), while California decides whether the person is qualified to apply pesticides and the county decides where, when, and under what local conditions. For the program's proposed spray track (AERO 140), the state layer is arguably the harder half, because it involves multiple exams, an apprenticeship year, and annual county paperwork that federal training programs elsewhere in the country never mention. [1] [2]

## The qualified applicator credentials

DPR's foundational applicator credentials are the Qualified Applicator Certificate (QAC) and the Qualified Applicator License (QAL). Both are earned by passing the Laws, Regulations, and Basic Principles core exam plus at least one pest control category exam, each with a minimum score of 70 percent, and both certify the holder to use or supervise the use of restricted-use pesticides in the categories held. The practical difference is commercial standing. The QAL is the higher credential, required to act as the qualifying person for a Pest Control Business License, the DPR license any business needs to apply pesticides for hire. The QAC qualifies an individual to apply and supervise restricted material applications, typically as an employee or for an employer's own operation, but does not qualify a business (with a narrow maintenance gardener exception). Categories run from A through M plus a landscape subcategory Q; for agricultural drone work the relevant category is Plant Agriculture, which UC ANR's California drone spray guide identifies as the category a new drone applicator should select alongside the core exam. Under the DPR fee table effective August 5, 2024, the QAC application fee is $90, the QAL application fee is $180, and each examination costs $115. [1] [3] [4] [5] [6]

## The Unmanned Pest Control Aircraft Pilot Certificate

California separately requires anyone who operates an aircraft to apply pesticides to hold a Pest Control Aircraft Pilot Certificate, a requirement that predates drones and was extended to them explicitly through DPR rulemaking 18-003, which split the certificate into Manned and Unmanned types. Like the manned certificate, the unmanned version comes in two levels, Apprentice and Journeyman. [2] [7]

A new Unmanned Apprentice Pilot Certificate applicant must submit proof of FAA authorization or certification to operate a UAS approved by the FAA to conduct pest control, which in practice means the Part 107 certificate plus the Part 137 exemption stack, and must pass two exams: the Laws, Regulations, and Basic Principles core exam and the Apprentice Pilot Certificate exam. The core exam is waived for holders of a current QAL or Agricultural Pest Control Adviser license, or for anyone who passed it within the previous year; DPR's instructions state specifically that the QAC does not satisfy the waiver. Under the current fee table, the application fee is $135, each exam is $115, and a $100 second-year fee issues the certificate for a two-year term. An apprentice can only work under supervision, and here DPR made a deliberate accommodation for the young industry: an unmanned apprentice may be supervised by either a manned or an unmanned journeyman, while a manned apprentice must be supervised by a manned journeyman. [2] [3] [6] [7]

The Journeyman certificate is the independent credential. A new journeyman applicant must document at least one year certified as an apprentice working under a journeyman, plus a minimum of 150 hours operating fixed-wing aircraft or 50 hours operating non-fixed-wing aircraft within the past two years, and must pass the Journeyman Pilot Certificate exam. The supervising journeyman signs a statement of supervision attesting to the dates and hours. This apprenticeship year is the state pipeline's bottleneck and a critical fact for career advising: a Cuesta graduate cannot walk out of a course and spray independently in California, but a graduate who has passed the exams and holds an apprentice certificate is exactly what an established spray business needs to hire. DPR also issues a separate Unmanned Vector Control Technician pilot subcategory for public health mosquito work, a niche worth mentioning but outside the agricultural track. DPR publishes suggested study materials for the pilot exams, including its Laws and Regulations Study Guide, the Pest Control Aircraft Pilot Study Guide, and the UC IPM volume The Safe and Effective Use of Pesticides, all natural candidate texts for AERO 140. [2] [7] [8]

## The county agricultural commissioner layer

Licensing is state business, but pesticide use is regulated day to day at the county level. A pest control business must register annually with the county agricultural commissioner in every county where it operates before performing work there. Growers and property operators obtain restricted materials permits from the commissioner, which function as site-specific and time-limited environmental review, and applications of restricted materials require a notice of intent filed with the commissioner in advance so the county can inspect or deny the specific application. Pesticide use reporting flows back through the same office monthly. In San Luis Obispo County these functions live in the Department of Agriculture/Weights and Measures, and most permit and notice transactions run through the statewide CalAgPermits system. For field exercises and guest speakers, the county office is a natural program partner, in the same way the FSDO is for airspace questions. [6] [9] [10]

## How the layers stack

A useful classroom exercise is to walk one hypothetical graduate through the full stack for spraying a Paso Robles vineyard with a 100 pound spray drone. Federally, they hold a Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate, a 49 U.S.C. 44807 exemption for the aircraft, an N-number registration, and an Agricultural Aircraft Operator Certificate under Part 137. At the state level, they hold a QAC in Plant Agriculture and an Unmanned Apprentice Pilot Certificate, and they work under a journeyman pilot for a DPR-licensed pest control business whose qualifying person holds a QAL. At the county level, the business is registered with the SLO County agricultural commissioner for the year, the vineyard holds any required restricted materials permit, a notice of intent precedes any restricted application, and use reports follow each job. Every layer is independent: no federal document substitutes for a state one, and no state license waives a county registration. That stacking logic, more than any single requirement, is the durable lesson for students, and it mirrors the layered airspace thinking taught elsewhere in the curriculum. [1] [2] [6] [9]

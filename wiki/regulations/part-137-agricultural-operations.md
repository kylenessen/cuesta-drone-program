# Part 137 Agricultural Operations

**Part 137** of Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Agricultural Aircraft Operations, is the FAA rule that governs dispensing substances from aircraft for agricultural purposes. It was written in 1966 for crewed crop dusters, and it requires anyone conducting an agricultural aircraft operation to hold an Agricultural Aircraft Operator Certificate (AAOC). A [Part 107](part-107.md) Remote Pilot Certificate alone does not authorize spraying or spreading from a drone, so Part 137 is the federal gate in front of the program's proposed agricultural spray track (AERO 140). Because the rule predates drones and assumes a pilot sitting in the aircraft, every drone spray operator today reaches Part 137 through an exemption process, and for aircraft over 55 pounds that process runs through the special statutory authority of 49 U.S.C. 44807. The state half of the spray operator's regulatory stack is covered in [California Pesticide Licensing](california-pesticide-licensing.md). [1] [2]

## What counts as an agricultural aircraft operation

Section 137.3 defines an agricultural aircraft operation as dispensing any economic poison, or dispensing any other substance intended for plant nourishment, soil treatment, propagation of plant life, or pest control, or engaging in dispensing activities directly affecting agriculture, horticulture, or forest preservation. The definition is broader than students usually expect. It is not limited to pesticides. Spreading dry fertilizer or cover crop seed from a drone is an agricultural aircraft operation, and the FAA has clarified that even chemicals used as disinfectants fall within the category of economic poisons. Part 137 distinguishes private operators, who treat their own crops, from commercial operators, who dispense for hire, with somewhat different pilot certification requirements for each. [1] [2] [3]

## Why a drone sprayer needs more than Part 107

Two separate problems stand between a certificated remote pilot and a legal spray job. First, section 107.36 prohibits carriage of hazardous materials under Part 107, which reaches most crop protection chemicals. Second, Part 137 itself was drafted for occupied aircraft, so a drone operator cannot comply with several of its provisions as written. The FAA resolves both problems through exemptions, and the path splits at the 55 pound line. [1]

A spray drone under 55 pounds at takeoff operates under Part 107 but needs an exemption from section 107.36 and from several Part 137 sections, plus registration through FAADroneZone. A spray drone at or over 55 pounds, which describes essentially every commercial agricultural platform in the proposal (the DJI Agras series and Hylio AG272 all exceed 55 pounds loaded), cannot operate under Part 107 at all. It operates under Parts 91 and 137, requires an exemption from regulations in Parts 61, 91, and 137, and must be registered with an N-number like a crewed aircraft. In both cases the operator must then obtain the AAOC itself, which cannot be waived. [1]

## The Section 44807 exemption

For the over 55 pound case, the FAA's authority to grant relief comes from 49 U.S.C. 44807, which lets the Administrator use a risk-based, case-by-case approach to decide whether certain unmanned aircraft may operate safely in the national airspace system without a standard airworthiness certificate. Congress most recently extended this authority in the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (Public Law 118-63), which pushed its expiration to September 30, 2033, and provided that exemptions in effect when the authority ends continue for up to three more years. [4] [5]

The petition itself is filed through the public docket at regulations.gov, at least 120 days before the exemption is needed, and must satisfy 14 CFR 11.81: identify the specific sections relief is sought from, explain the extent of and reason for the relief, and justify why granting it is in the public interest and provides an equivalent level of safety. Petitions are supported by documents such as operations manuals, safety risk analyses, and maintenance procedures, and the FAA warns that incomplete petitions may simply be closed. Before an exemption issues, the applicant must also supply the operator's legal name and address, a chief supervisor of operations, the pilot in command's certificate number, and the make, model, and registration of each aircraft. Standard exemption conditions require the remote pilot in command to hold a Part 107 certificate, and UC ANR's California guide additionally lists an FAA third-class medical certificate for aircraft over 55 pounds. Because thousands of near-identical spray drone petitions have now been processed, the package for common platforms is well worn, and equipment vendors routinely sell prepared petition templates. [1] [4] [6]

## The streamlined Part 137 certificate

Once the exemption is granted, the AAOC application has been deliberately simplified for UAS. Applicants no longer work through their local Flight Standards District Office. Instead they complete FAA Form 8710-3 and email their exemption number to UAS137Certificates@faa.gov, with the applicant name matching the exemption holder exactly. The streamlined certificate covers visual line of sight operations within the altitude and airspeed limits of the exemption. Flying a spray mission beyond visual line of sight would additionally require relief from section 107.31 or the framework in pending [Part 108 BVLOS Rulemaking](part-108-bvlos-rulemaking.md), which is one reason waiver literacy from [Certificate of Waiver](certificate-of-waiver.md) carries over to agricultural work. The program's certificate proposal budgets two to four months for the whole approval sequence, which is consistent with the FAA's 120 day petition lead time. [1]

## Knowledge and skill requirements

Section 137.19(e) requires an AAOC applicant to demonstrate knowledge and skill in agricultural aircraft operations. The knowledge areas read like a syllabus for a spray course: steps to be taken before starting operations, including survey of the area to be worked; safe handling of economic poisons and proper disposal of used containers; the general effects of economic poisons and agricultural chemicals on plants, animals, and persons; primary symptoms of poisoning and appropriate emergency measures; performance capabilities and operating limitations of the aircraft; and safe flight and application procedures. The skill portion, by contrast, was written for someone flying an airplane or helicopter, with maneuvers like short-field takeoffs, flare-outs, swath runs, pullups and turnarounds, and quick stops. UAS petitions therefore request relief from the manned-specific portions of the test, while the knowledge subject matter remains the substance the FAA expects an agricultural operator to command. For curriculum design, 137.19(e) knowledge areas plus drift management and calibration are the natural core of AERO 140's regulatory and safety content. [3]

## Growth, enforcement, and modernization

Agricultural spraying has quietly become the dominant use of heavy drones in the United States. In the preamble discussion around the 2025 BVLOS proposal, the FAA stated that over 1,700 Part 137-related UAS exemptions and operator certificates had been issued, while the Ag Drone Initiative, an industry group, estimates the real population of heavy-lift agricultural drone operators at 10,000 or more, implying a compliance rate near 20 percent. That gap explains the FAA's current enforcement posture: the agency has publicly noted exemption holders operating outside their conditions and limitations and has begun operational validations and Requests for Information to verify compliance. A college program that teaches the full legal stack is directly addressing the industry's most visible weakness. [1] [6]

Modernization is active on two fronts. Congress extended 44807 through 2033, as noted above, signaling that the exemption pathway remains the bridge until purpose-built rules exist. Meanwhile the FAA's Part 108 notice of proposed rulemaking, Normalizing Unmanned Aircraft Systems Beyond Visual Line of Sight Operations, published August 7, 2025, would create the first standing framework that touches agricultural drones directly. The proposal would except Part 108 aircraft from Part 137's applicability, acknowledges that Part 137 is an imperfect fit for UAS, and would allow agricultural dispensing only under its more demanding certificated operations tier rather than the lighter permit tier, a limitation agricultural groups opposed in comments. The comment period closed October 6, 2025 with more than 3,000 responses, and as of mid 2026 the final rule had not yet published. Until it does, the exemption plus AAOC pathway described on this page remains the law, and even after Part 108 arrives, the FAA has signaled that a UAS-specific successor to Part 137 would require its own future rulemaking. [6] [7] [8]

## What this means for a teaching program

Three practical points follow for Cuesta's planning. First, the aircraft is regulated before the payload: a 100 pound Agras cannot legally fly at all without a 44807 exemption, even loaded with plain water, because it is too heavy for Part 107. Training flights dispensing only water arguably fall outside the 137.3 definition of an agricultural operation, but the college would still need exemption coverage, insurance, and procedures for the aircraft itself, or it would need to train on a sub-55-pound platform. Second, the credential belongs to the operator, not the school. Students leave the course positioned to petition for their own exemptions and certificates, so the paperwork itself, the petition, the operations manual, and the conditions and limitations, is legitimate course content rather than administrative overhead. Third, Part 137 is only the federal half. A graduate who holds a Part 107 certificate, a 44807 exemption, and an AAOC still cannot legally apply pesticides in California without the state licensing described in [California Pesticide Licensing](california-pesticide-licensing.md). [1] [6]

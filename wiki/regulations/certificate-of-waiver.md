# Certificate of Waiver

A **Certificate of Waiver** (CoW) is the FAA authorization, established in Subpart D of [Part 107](/regulations/part-107.md), that permits a small UAS operation to deviate from specific operating rules when the Administrator finds the proposed operation can be conducted safely under the certificate's terms. Waivers are how commercial operators today fly beyond visual line of sight, at night without standard lighting, over people outside the category system, or with multiple aircraft under one pilot. The knowledge test treats waiver policy lightly, with a single ACS knowledge element, but professionally it is a major topic because it defines the ceiling of what a certificated pilot can be hired to do. [1](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](/sources/ac-107-2a.md)

## Requirements

Section 107.200 authorizes the FAA to waive the following rules, listed in section 107.205 [2](/sources/ac-107-2a.md):

- Section 107.25, operation from a moving vehicle or aircraft
- Section 107.29(a)(2) and (b), anti-collision lighting for night and civil twilight operations
- Section 107.31, visual line of sight (never waivable for carrying another person's property for compensation or hire)
- Section 107.33, visual observer requirements
- Section 107.35, operation of multiple small unmanned aircraft
- Section 107.37(a), yielding the right of way
- Section 107.39, operation over people
- Section 107.41, operation in certain airspace
- Section 107.51, the operating limitations for speed, altitude, visibility, and cloud clearance
- Section 107.145, operations over moving vehicles

The application, filed through the FAA's DroneZone portal, must completely describe the proposed operation and justify, with supporting data where necessary, that it can be conducted safely under the waiver's terms. The FAA publishes Waiver Safety Explanation Guidelines to structure that safety case, and encourages filing at least 60 days before the planned operation, which is also its target adjudication time. In practice, applicants are describing mitigations: how they will maintain awareness of the aircraft without direct sight, what technology substitutes for the waived rule, and how failures are handled. [2](/sources/ac-107-2a.md)

One nuance worth teaching: the sparsely populated area condition for operating from a moving vehicle is itself waivable, and the FAA notes that even a categorical-sounding rule can carry a waivable subprovision. Conversely, some things cannot be waived at all, such as the 55-pound aircraft weight ceiling that defines Part 107's applicability. [2](/sources/ac-107-2a.md)

The waiver era for beyond visual line of sight flight may be ending: the pending [Part 108 BVLOS Rulemaking](/regulations/part-108-bvlos-rulemaking.md) proposes replacing per-operation waivers with a standing regulatory framework.

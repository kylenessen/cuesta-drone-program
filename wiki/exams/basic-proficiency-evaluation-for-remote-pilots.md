# Basic Proficiency Evaluation for Remote Pilots

The **Basic Proficiency Evaluation for Remote Pilots** (BPERP) is the compact, entry-level version of the NIST drone flight test, and the name of the credential awarded for passing it. Where the full [NIST Open Test Lane](nist-open-test-lane.md) is a line of many stands, the BPERP reduces the same scoring method to three omni bucket stands that can be flown in about ten minutes with a tape measure and a stopwatch [1] [2]. The Airborne Public Safety Association issues a BPERP certificate to pilots who pass it under a proctor, layered on top of the FAA [Part 107 Knowledge Test](part-107-knowledge-test.md), which makes it the clearest existing model for what AERO 100 is trying to build: an awardable flying credential stacked on the knowledge certificate [1] [3].

The BPERP matters here for two reasons beyond the credential itself. It is the specific test used in the published benchmark data that should shape the AERO 100 flight rubric, and it presents a live design choice about whether the course capstone should target this short standard credential rather than the fuller Level 2 lane. Both are developed below.

## The test

The BPERP compresses the Open Test Lane's method into a minimal apparatus. It uses three standard omni bucket stands, a 50 foot tape measure to set them out, and a stopwatch, and a proctor can administer it in roughly ten minutes [2]. The pilot flies a short set of maneuvers past the stands and captures the sticker targets inside the buckets, scoring points for the features the camera resolves, exactly as in the full lane [1]. Research on the test found that a simplified BPERP using only its first two maneuvers predicts overall performance about as well as the full maneuver set, which is why a resource-limited program can run the short version without losing measurement value [4]. The full method, apparatus, and scoring are documented in [NIST Open Test Lane](nist-open-test-lane.md); this page covers the short evaluation and the credential built on it.

## The APSA certificate

The Airborne Public Safety Association is a long-established public safety aviation nonprofit, and its BPERP certificate is the most recognized credential built directly on the NIST method [1] [3]. The certificate requires a current Part 107 remote pilot certificate as its entry ticket, which positions it as a flying credential added on top of the FAA's knowledge test rather than a replacement for it [1]. APSA delivers training and evaluation in cooperation with Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University and public safety partners including AIRT and DroneResponders, and it offers proctor and advanced-proctor courses so agencies and schools can administer the evaluation themselves [3] [5]. The credential emerged from the public safety sector, where the National Fire Protection Association's NFPA 2400 standard references NIST-style job performance requirements, and it remains anchored there even as commercial and academic programs adopt it [6].

## Benchmark data

The most useful published data for setting a proficiency cutoff comes from a 2023 study by Colin Dees and Joseph Burgett at Clemson University, which analyzed BPERP results to give two-year colleges a data-driven benchmark instead of an arbitrary time [4]. The authors built percentile-based flight-time tiers, placing the top ten percent of pilots at or under five minutes and nine seconds and the median around seven to eight minutes [4]. Their central finding is the one that should shape any NIST-based rubric. Ninety-three percent of participants passed the image-scoring portion of the test, but only forty-eight percent met the time requirement, meaning the clock, not image quality, is what actually separates pilots [4]. The practical implication for AERO 100 is that the time limit is the real difficulty knob and the drill progression should be built around working faster rather than merely cleaner. One caution applies when carrying these numbers over: they describe the short BPERP specifically, so the five-minute-nine-second mark cannot be transplanted onto the Level 2 lane's 25 minute allowance without its own data. The programs that use these results are surveyed in [NIST Flight Proficiency Programs](../analysis/nist-flight-proficiency-programs.md).

## Academic adoption

The BPERP is already used as a course credential at the community college level, which is the closest available precedent for the AERO 100 plan. Carroll Community College in Maryland runs a commercial drone pilot certificate that explicitly prepares students for two credentials, the FAA Part 107 certificate and the BPERP certificate, and has students fly the NIST test lane to demonstrate control and accuracy across several Saturdays of hands-on flight training blended with classroom and simulator work [7]. That is essentially the shape Cuesta is reaching for, an FAA knowledge credential paired with a NIST flying credential inside one course, and it is worth a direct look as the capstone design firms up. Making a local capstone score portable in the same way runs through proctor certification, the credentialing path detailed in [NIST Flight Proficiency Programs](../analysis/nist-flight-proficiency-programs.md).

## Relevance to AERO 100

The approved AERO 100 outline currently names the Level 2 [NIST Open Test Lane](nist-open-test-lane.md) flown in 25 minutes as its flight outcome [8]. The BPERP raises a design question worth settling deliberately rather than by default. Aligning the capstone to the short BPERP would let student results plug directly into an existing, recognized certificate and into the Clemson benchmark data, at the cost of testing a narrower slice of skill than the full five-path lane. Keeping the Level 2 lane tests more of the flying that mapping and inspection work demands, but produces a score that lives only on the transcript unless the program separately pursues APSA alignment. The tradeoff belongs in the capstone conversation and is recorded here rather than resolved.

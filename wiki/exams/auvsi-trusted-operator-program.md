# AUVSI Trusted Operator Program

The **AUVSI Trusted Operator Program** (TOP) is a voluntary certification standard for drone pilots run by the Association for Uncrewed Vehicle Systems International. It exists to fill the gap the [Part 107 Knowledge Test](part-107-knowledge-test.md) leaves open. Part 107 is a knowledge test with no flight assessment, so it proves someone may fly commercially, not that they can fly well. TOP layers a graded training standard and, at its upper levels, an in-person flight check on top of the FAA certificate [1] [3]. For Cuesta's curriculum the program matters less as a credential to award than as a syllabus to mine. Its training standard describes what a professional operator course should teach at each tier of operational risk, and its flight assessment describes what a defensible flight check looks like. The program's position is to make trusted operators whether AUVSI ever recognizes them or not, so this page documents the standard as raw material, with the organizational interface flagged separately as a program development option.

## The Levels

TOP defines three certification levels, each scoped to the complexity and hazard of the operation rather than to a body of knowledge alone [1]. Level 1 covers routine Part 107 operations, meaning daytime flight under 400 feet within visual line of sight, no waivers, electric aircraft under five pounds, and a low complexity safety management footprint [2] [3] [4]. Level 2 builds on Level 1 and covers operations that require a Part 107 waiver or a crew, meaning visual observers or sensor operators, and operations with elevated risk factors such as proximity to airports, helipads, populated areas, people, and livestock [2] [4] [8]. Contemporary coverage of the program's launch framed Level 2 explicitly around work near infrastructure like cell towers, power lines, and wind turbines [3]. Level 3 covers safety critical environments, including chemical, oil, gas, nuclear, and mining facilities, power plants and offshore rigs, infrastructure inspection of wind turbines, powerlines, communication towers, and pipelines, and hazardous settings such as ships, moving vehicles, severe weather, and remote locations, along with aircraft over 55 pounds and the development of novel procedures [2] [4] [7]. Level 3 flight assessment is sector specific, reflecting for example the distinct requirements of the electrical industry [3]. All three levels require a current Part 107 certificate as the entry ticket, and each level requires the one below it [4] [5].

## The Protocol Certification Manual

The document behind the program is the Protocol Certification Manual, or PCM. Research notes sometimes expand PCM as Performance Compliance Matrix, but AUVSI's own materials consistently use Protocol Certification Manual, and no document under the other name surfaced in this research. The PCM defines a protocol as a specified level of knowledge, ability, performance, or compliance, and certification at a given level means demonstrating conformance with every protocol applicable to that level [1]. The manual points to more than 300 accredited standards, policies, procedures, best practices, and codes of conduct across the three levels, drawing on bodies including ISO, ASTM, ANSI, NFPA, OSHA, RTCA, ICAO, and IEEE [1] [12]. It was developed by a steering committee of more than 170 subject matter experts from over 50 organizations and is updated on a two year cycle [3]. AUVSI distributes the PCM through a request form rather than an open download, so this page documents its requirements through the accredited training providers who teach to it, and the manual itself remains worth obtaining if the program ever moves toward the provider pathway [1].

## What the Training Covers

Provider catalogs are the clearest public window into the curriculum. NC State's Institute for Transportation Research and Education teaches Level 1 as eight modules and twelve instructional hours covering airmanship principles, air law, UAS operation, flight principles, airspace navigation, meteorology, human performance, emergency planning, and night operations [4]. Its Level 2 course is six modules and sixteen instructional hours plus an in-person flight evaluation, adding crew resource management, emergency response, checklists, documentation, and human factors [4]. Its Level 3 course adds first aid and bleeding control, air boss responsibilities, safety management systems, spectrum analysis, and expanded theoretical knowledge [4]. DARTdrones structures its offering as fourteen online micro courses, including site assessment, real world airspace, weather assessment, battery care and safety, and air law, plus a day and a half of in-person instruction covering preflight planning, real world operations, aerial inspection techniques, and managing drone emergencies [9].

A community college syllabus from NCAT's partner network shows Level 2 taught as a three credit course in six content modules, Operational Overview, Navigation, Meteorology and Environmental Elements, Human Factors, Personal Flight Assessment, and Quality Assurance, with objectives reaching into UAS architecture, data links and frequency propagation, and payloads including electro optical, infrared, and synthetic aperture radar sensors [10] [11]. Embry-Riddle's Level 2 competency list adds the operational paperwork layer, requiring candidates to identify a safe and legal flight training area, analyze location and aircraft using a sUAS preflight risk assessment form, explain the benefits of record keeping, demonstrate mastery of flight maneuvers, perform ground control station operations, and execute an autonomous flight safely [6]. The pattern across providers is consistent. The knowledge layer largely restates Part 107 ground school, and the value TOP adds sits in the operational discipline layer, meaning risk assessment forms, crew coordination, documentation, quality assurance, and the flight check itself.

## The Practical Flight Assessment

Levels 2 and 3 require a Practical Flight Assessment, or PFA, delivered in person or, at some providers, remotely by video with instructor approval [4] [5]. Embry-Riddle's course pages give the most concrete picture of what the PFA involves. Flight standards follow ASTM F3266, the standard guide for training remote pilots, and the assessment apparatus is built to NIST standards, meaning the same bucket stand test lane infrastructure documented in [Flight Proficiency and Practice Exercises](../topics/flight-proficiency-and-practice-exercises.md) [6] [7]. Candidates fly a commercial off the shelf aircraft with a gimbaled RGB camera, must present a current remote pilot certificate and pilot logbook, and are warned that passing is not guaranteed [6] [7]. Certification at Level 2 additionally requires verification of training markers and a completed TOP compliance worksheet alongside the PFA itself [8], and the NCAT syllabus grades its terminal exercise as a check-ride style application of everything in the course [11].

For AERO 100 this is the central finding. The TOP flight assessment and the course's existing NIST test lane capstone are built on the same apparatus and the same measurement philosophy, so AERO 100 already contains the core of a TOP style PFA. What TOP adds around the lane is the check-ride framing, meaning the flight is one scored element inside a larger assessment that also covers a preflight risk assessment, documentation and logbook review, and ground control station and autonomous flight execution.

## Recertification and Cost

TOP certification is not permanent. Pilots at every level recertify every two years, and Levels 2 and 3 recertification includes a repeat Practical Flight Assessment [5] [13]. Embry-Riddle prices initial certification at $499 for Level 1 and $599 each for Levels 2 and 3, with recertification courses at $99 per level, certification fees included [5]. NC State ITRE, as a university provider, charges $260 for Level 1, $550 for Level 2, and $700 for Level 3 at general rates, with steep discounts for public safety, military, and government personnel, free enrollment for its own students, and a workforce development rate of $160 covering Levels 1 and 2 together for students and educators [4]. Those academic price points are worth noting because they show what a college can charge for this material when it becomes a provider.

## Market Recognition

Honest notes on recognition matter more here than for the FAA and thermography credentials, because TOP's value is uneven. AUVSI reports more than 2,500 Trusted Operator certificates issued across 31 countries since the program launched in 2019 [1] [3], which is a small population against the hundreds of thousands of Part 107 certificate holders. Recognition concentrates where the program was aimed, in the utility, energy, and enterprise inspection sectors. The launch coverage framed the program around utility and energy operations, Level 3 assessments are tailored to sectors like the electrical industry [3], and energy industry resources present TOP as the assurance layer customers should look for when hiring operators [12]. Tangible third party recognition is modest but real. Global Aerospace recognizes the certification for potential insurance benefits [2], and NC State advertises a five percent drone insurance discount for its graduates [4]. Outside those circles most employers have never heard of the program, and job postings rarely name it, so the working assumption for curriculum planning is that TOP is a source of standards and structure, not a credential students need. Whether the energy sector partners Cuesta cares about actually value it is an open question assigned to the PG&E partnership conversation, as noted below.

## The Training Provider Pathway

The organizational interface, if the program ever wants it, runs through instructors and provider accreditation. A TOP Remote Pilot Instructor must hold at least a Level 2 Trusted Operator certification and meet additional PCM requirements, and instructors may only train students up to the level they themselves hold [2]. Instructors recertify on the same two year cycle, including a repeat flight assessment [13]. Training providers are accredited by AUVSI against curriculum quality, instructor qualifications, safety management, and adherence to best practices, teach from AUVSI approved courseware, and are then authorized to issue Trusted Operator certifications to their students [1] [2].

NCAT, the NSF funded National Center for Autonomous Technology, runs a mentor and protege model built exactly for schools like Cuesta. NCAT partners with institutions, trains their drone educators to become Trusted Operator Level 2 Remote Pilot Instructors, and those instructors then certify their own students as Level 2 service providers, with NCAT's accredited provider status absorbing the cost, documentation, and staffing barriers that keep individual schools out [10]. Its partner network already teaches Level 2 as a three credit community college course [11]. For Cuesta this is a plausible expansion for the utility inspection track, flagged here as a program development idea rather than a plan. It is only worth pursuing if the energy sector partners actually value the credential, and that question belongs in the PG&E partnership conversation. If PG&E and its contractors do not care about TOP, the right move is to keep mining the curriculum and skip the organizational interface entirely.

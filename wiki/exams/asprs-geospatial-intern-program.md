# ASPRS Geospatial Intern Program

The **ASPRS Geospatial Intern Program** is the student on-ramp to certification by the American Society for Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing (ASPRS), the professional society for photogrammetry, remote sensing, and the mapping sciences. Full ASPRS certifications, including the [Certified UAS Technologist](/exams/certified-uas-technologist.md), require years of professional experience that no student has. The intern program removes exactly that barrier. A student still enrolled applies with one faculty reference and a transcript, sits the same certification examination the professionals take, and on passing holds an intern designation from a certification program accredited by the Council of Engineering and Scientific Specialty Boards [1] [3]. The designation then matures into full certification once the graduate accumulates the required experience on the job, with no second examination.

For Cuesta's drone program this is the mechanism that could let a graduating student leave with a national professional credential already in hand, earned against coursework from [AERO 100](/sources/aero-100-course-outline.md) and the mapping course. The program's written record has a significant gap, however. The current edition of the ASPRS certification guidelines no longer describes the intern program, so the mechanics below rest on the 2019 guidelines and an archived ASPRS application page, and several facts need confirmation directly from ASPRS before the pathway is promised to students [1] [2] [3].

## How the Program Works

ASPRS created the pathway in May 2006 as provisional certification, designed to assist students during their transition to professional status, and later renamed it the Geospatial Intern Program [2]. An applicant must be an active student preparing to graduate in a degree program with training in the spatial sciences from an institution of higher education. The application package is an application form and fee, one reference from an academic advisor or relevant faculty member, an official transcript, and a declaration of compliance with the ASPRS Code of Ethics [3]. The archived application page states the transcript must validate a minimum of two courses in the spatial sciences, naming photogrammetry, remote sensing, and GIS/LIS as the qualifying subjects, while the 2019 guidelines state the threshold as twelve credits in the geospatial sciences [2] [3]. The two versions plainly aim at the same idea, but which wording governs today is not confirmed.

The application goes to ASPRS Headquarters for peer review by the Evaluation for Certification Committee. Applicants who pass peer review are given the opportunity to take the appropriate certification examination within six months of their notification date [3]. Passing the exam earns intern-level recognition immediately. The intern does not hold the word "Certified", which ASPRS reserves for the full credential, but the designation is real and public. The 2019 guidelines list "Geospatial Intern (ASPRS)" among the designations that may be used on business stationery and cards, and the archived application page names category-specific titles such as Photogrammetric Technologist Intern [2] [3]. A March 2026 university overview of geospatial certifications describes the program as current and open to graduating seniors, and the live ASPRS certification page still names interns in the program's purpose statement, so the program appears active despite its absence from the current guidelines [4] [6].

## The Examination

The intern sits the same examination as a full certification candidate for the chosen specialty. All ASPRS certification exams are computer-based tests administered through Prometric, taken either at a Prometric testing center or by remote proctoring, with results delivered immediately [1]. Technologist examinations run no longer than two hours, against four hours for the Scientist level [1]. Prometric charges its own testing fee on top of the ASPRS application fee [1].

The published content matrix for the Certified UAS Technologist exam is roughly 70 percent UAS subject matter, with the remainder spread evenly across mathematics, earth science, engineering and surveying, physics, and professional and ethical practice at about 6 percent each [1]. That mix sits squarely on the knowledge a drone mapping curriculum teaches, from [Structure from Motion](/topics/structure-from-motion.md) and [Mapping Accuracy](/topics/mapping-accuracy.md) through [Part 107 Operating Rules](/regulations/part-107-operating-rules.md). An approved applicant who fails the first attempt may retake the exam once within six months, paying only the Prometric testing fee again. A second failure requires a full reapplication with fees and updated references [1].

## From Intern to Certified UAS Technologist

The intern designation is built to convert. A Technologist Intern has five years from the examination date to complete the three years of qualifying experience the full credential requires, of which two years must be in the specialty category. A Scientist-track intern has ten years to complete the six year requirement. Extensions are granted only for active duty military service [1] [3]. When the experience is complete, the intern submits written documentation and three references to ASPRS Headquarters, the Evaluation for Certification Committee reviews the package, and the applicant becomes fully certified for the normal certification period without retesting [3].

Educational credit shortens the experience clock. The current guidelines allow a degree to count for half a year of experience in the Technologist track, listing bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees [1]. A Department of Defense credentialing profile for an ASPRS technologist certification states that an associate or higher degree counts one half year toward total time, which would apply directly to a Cuesta associate degree, but the guidelines themselves do not name associate degrees, so this needs confirmation [7]. Under that reading, a graduate working in drone mapping could reach full Certified UAS Technologist roughly two and a half years into their first job, comfortably inside the five year window even with a slow start or a career detour.

ASPRS certification is specialty recognition by professional peers, not a license. It does not substitute for state licensure where licensure applies, a distinction that matters in mapping work and is developed in [Drone Mapping and Licensed Surveying](/topics/drone-mapping-and-licensed-surveying.md) [1].

## Fees

The current guidelines price initial Technologist certification at $200 for ASPRS members and $375 for non-members, but their fee table carries no Geospatial Intern rows [1]. The 2019 guidelines did: an initial intern application of $200 for members and $250 for non-members, and a final application at conversion of $100 and $200 [2]. An earlier third-party overview of the program quotes still different intern figures, consistent with fees changing over the years [5]. The honest current answer is that the intern application fee is unpublished and must be asked of ASPRS directly. Prometric's separate testing fee applies in any case [1].

## Precedent at Other Institutions

Universities do route students toward the program, though published examples are thin. The University of Arkansas lists the Geospatial Intern Program certification first among the external certifications its online geospatial technologies certificates prepare students for, at both the undergraduate certificate of proficiency level and the graduate certificate level [8] [9]. University career and certification guides, including one published by USC in March 2026, present the intern program as the standard student entry into ASPRS certification [4]. Several universities host ASPRS student chapters that feed the same pipeline.

No community college publicly documenting a Geospatial Intern pathway was found in this research pass, and no published pass rates or student outcome data for the program were found anywhere. The absence cuts both ways for the leadership pitch. There is no proof text from a peer institution, and there is also a plain first-mover opening: a community college drone program that routes students through a CESB-accredited national credential would be documenting something no peer appears to have documented. Department of Defense credentialing programs list ASPRS technologist certifications as in-demand credentials eligible for military credentialing assistance, which adds a veteran-serving argument to the same pitch [7].

## The Cuesta Student Journey

The pathway the program design intends looks like this. A student completes AERO 100 and the drone mapping course, which together deliver the UAS, photogrammetry, and remote sensing knowledge the exam matrix tests. In the final semester the student applies as a Geospatial Intern with the course instructor as the faculty reference, pays the application fee, and submits a transcript showing the qualifying coursework. After peer review the student schedules a two hour Prometric exam inside the six month window, timed so the coursework is fresh. The student graduates holding an ASPRS intern designation, starts work in drone mapping or inspection, and converts to Certified UAS Technologist a few years later by documenting the experience with three references, without ever retesting.

Two fit questions need answers before this is promised in course materials. First, whether the drone courses qualify as spatial sciences coursework under the transcript rule. The mapping course plainly teaches photogrammetry and remote sensing, but AERO 100 is aeronautical knowledge, and if it does not count the program may need a second qualifying course such as a GIS offering. Second, whether a student completing a certificate rather than an associate degree meets the enrolled-and-preparing-to-graduate eligibility rule. Both belong on the agenda for the planned ASPRS outreach call.

## Open Questions for the ASPRS Call

The following could not be confirmed from available sources and should be resolved directly with ASPRS.

1. Is the Geospatial Intern Program active and accepting applications in its documented form, given that the current certification guidelines no longer describe it?
2. Does the intern pathway cover the UAS Technologist category? The archived application page lists only the six pre-UAS intern designations [3].
3. What is the current intern application fee, and is there still a separate final application fee at conversion? The current fee table lists neither [1] [2].
4. What is the current transcript threshold, two courses or twelve credits, and would Cuesta's AERO courses qualify as spatial sciences coursework [2] [3]?
5. Does a student finishing a certificate program, rather than an associate degree, meet the eligibility rule of an active student preparing to graduate [3]?
6. Does an associate degree earn the half year of experience credit toward the technologist requirement [1] [7]?
7. Can a student cohort test together, for example through a proctored group sitting arranged with the college or at an ASPRS event, or does Prometric handle every candidate as an individual appointment [1]?
8. What is the correct resume form of the designation for a UAS-track intern, Geospatial Intern (ASPRS) or a category title such as UAS Technologist Intern [2] [3]?
9. Does ASPRS have existing relationships, agreements, or informal precedent with community colleges, and are any pass rate or outcome figures available for student intern candidates?

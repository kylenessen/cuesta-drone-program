# Night Operations

**Night Operations** under Part 107 became routine in April 2021, when the FAA replaced the old daylight-only rule and its waiver process with two conditions: the remote pilot in command must have current knowledge training covering night operations, and the aircraft must carry lighted anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles with a flash rate sufficient to avoid collision. Night is defined as the time between the end of evening civil twilight and the beginning of morning civil twilight, as published in The Air Almanac. This topic was added to the ACS in 2021 and threads through several test areas, from operating rules to physiology. [1](/sources/ac-107-2a.md) [2](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

## Requirements and Practice

Civil twilight, in the contiguous United States, runs for 30 minutes after sunset and 30 minutes before sunrise (Alaska follows The Air Almanac's definition). During civil twilight the same 3-statute-mile anti-collision lighting is required, and at night it must be on and functioning. The remote pilot may reduce the light's intensity or visible range only when it would be in the interest of safety, for example to preserve the pilot's own night vision, but remains responsible for collision avoidance. The lighting requirement, though not the training requirement, can be waived through a [Certificate of Waiver](/regulations/certificate-of-waiver.md). [1](/sources/ac-107-2a.md)

The training condition is satisfied by the post-April 2021 initial knowledge test or the current online recurrent training, so every newly certificated student automatically holds night privileges, a point worth making explicitly in class since older guides still describe night waivers. Preflight for night adds verifying the anti-collision light works and that battery planning accounts for its draw, as covered in [Maintenance and Inspection](/topics/maintenance-and-inspection.md). [1](/sources/ac-107-2a.md) [3](https://www.faa.gov/uas/commercial_operators/become_a_drone_pilot)

Operating at night also demands the visual physiology covered in [Remote Pilot Physiology](/topics/remote-pilot-physiology.md): dark adaptation takes about 30 minutes, off-center viewing outperforms direct staring, and illusions like autokinesis can mislead the pilot about the aircraft's motion. Site assessment must happen in daylight, because unlighted hazards (wires, poles, terrain) are invisible later, and the ACS tests awareness of ground structures, their lighting, and manned aircraft lighting, which lets a remote pilot infer another aircraft's direction of flight from its position lights. Flying over people at night layers the [Operations Over People](/regulations/operations-over-people.md) requirements on top of these. [2](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

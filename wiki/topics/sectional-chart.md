# Sectional Chart

A **Sectional Chart** is the standard VFR aeronautical chart, drawn at 1:500,000 scale, that depicts airspace, airports, navigation aids, obstacles, and terrain. It is the remote pilot's primary tool for answering the question the FAA cares most about: what airspace am I in, and what does it require? A large share of [Part 107 Knowledge Test](/exams/part-107-knowledge-test.md) airspace questions present a sectional excerpt from the testing supplement and ask the applicant to interpret it, so chart reading deserves dedicated practice time in class rather than a single lecture pass. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md) [2](/sources/uag-sample-questions.md)

## Reading the Chart

Airspace classes appear as colored boundaries: Class B in solid blue lines, Class C in solid magenta, Class D in dashed blue with the ceiling in a bracketed box, Class E to the surface in dashed magenta, and the 700-foot Class E floor as a shaded magenta vignette. Towered airports print in blue and nontowered airports in magenta, with airport data blocks giving the identifier, elevation, runway length, and the frequencies covered under [Radio Communications](/topics/radio-communications.md). [Airspace Classification](/topics/airspace-classification.md) covers what each class requires; the chart is where students learn to see it. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

Test items regularly require reading latitude and longitude to locate a point (degrees and minutes along the chart edges), identifying obstacles from their symbols with both MSL and AGL heights printed beside them (the AGL figure in parentheses), and using the maximum elevation figure printed in each quadrangle. Special use airspace boundaries, military training routes, and parachute operation symbols also appear. The chart legend resolves any symbol, and the FAA permits using the testing supplement's legend during the exam. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md) [2](/sources/uag-sample-questions.md)

Charts are revised on a fixed cycle, so pilots must fly with current data; stale charts miss new towers and airspace changes. In field practice most pilots read the same data through apps that overlay sectional graphics, but the test assumes the paper representation. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

The companion reference is the Chart Supplement, the airport directory giving details a chart cannot hold, such as full frequency lists, hours of tower operation, and airport remarks. ACS airport-data questions expect students to know which facts live on the chart and which live in the supplement. [3](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

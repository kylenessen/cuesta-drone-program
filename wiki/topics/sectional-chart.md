# Sectional Chart

A **Sectional Chart** is the standard VFR aeronautical chart, drawn at 1:500,000 scale, that depicts airspace, airports, navigation aids, obstacles, and terrain. It is the remote pilot's primary tool for answering the question the FAA cares most about: what airspace am I in, and what does it require? A large share of [Part 107 Knowledge Test](../exams/part-107-knowledge-test.md) airspace questions present a sectional excerpt from the testing supplement and ask the applicant to interpret it, so chart reading deserves dedicated practice time in class rather than a single lecture pass. ## Reading the Chart

Airspace classes appear as colored boundaries: Class B in solid blue lines, Class C in solid magenta, Class D in dashed blue with the ceiling in a bracketed box, Class E to the surface in dashed magenta, and the 700-foot Class E floor as a shaded magenta vignette. Towered airports print in blue and nontowered airports in magenta, with airport data blocks giving the identifier, elevation, runway length, and the frequencies covered under [Radio Communications](radio-communications.md). [Airspace Classification](airspace-classification.md) covers what each class requires; the chart is where students learn to see it. [1](../sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

Test items regularly require identifying obstacles from their symbols with both MSL and AGL heights printed beside them (the AGL figure in parentheses), and using the maximum elevation figure printed in each quadrangle. Special use airspace boundaries, military training routes, and parachute operation symbols also appear. The chart legend resolves any symbol, and the FAA permits using the testing supplement's legend during the exam. [1](../sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md) [2](../sources/uag-sample-questions.md)

Charts are revised on a fixed cycle, so pilots must fly with current data; stale charts miss new towers and airspace changes. In field practice most pilots read the same data through apps that overlay sectional graphics, but the test assumes the paper representation. [1](../sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

The companion reference is the Chart Supplement, the airport directory giving details a chart cannot hold, such as full frequency lists, hours of tower operation, and airport remarks; it is covered with the rest of the reference publications in [Aeronautical Publications and References](aeronautical-publications-and-references.md). ACS airport-data questions expect students to know which facts live on the chart and which live in the supplement. [3](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

## Geographical Coordinates

The chart's location language is latitude and longitude. Parallels of latitude circle the earth parallel to the equator and measure degrees north or south of it; meridians of longitude run pole to pole and measure degrees east or west of the Prime Meridian through Greenwich, England. The conterminous United States sits between 25 and 49 degrees north latitude and 67 and 125 degrees west longitude, so every mainland US position reads as north latitude and west longitude, with each degree divided into 60 minutes. Test questions give a coordinate pair and ask the applicant to find the point using the degree and minute ticks along the chart's edges and graticule lines, then answer something about what is there, so plotting coordinates deserves hands-on practice. A related chart fact worth a mention in lecture is magnetic variation, the angle between true and magnetic north drawn on the chart as dashed magenta isogonic lines, which explains why a charted runway heading and a compass disagree. Coordinates on the chart connect forward to the mapping half of the program: [Map Projections and Coordinate Systems](map-projections-and-coordinate-systems.md) picks up latitude and longitude where the chart leaves off.

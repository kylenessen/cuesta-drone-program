# Emergency Procedures

**Emergency Procedures** covers how a remote pilot plans for and responds to things going wrong: battery fires, loss of the control link, flyaways, GPS loss, and interference. The regulatory backstop is that in an in-flight emergency the remote pilot in command may deviate from any rule of Part 107 to the extent required to meet the emergency, reporting to the FAA afterward if requested. The teaching emphasis, and the ACS's, is that emergencies are handled well when they were briefed before takeoff, which connects this topic to [Aeronautical Decision-Making](aeronautical-decision-making.md). ## Contingency Planning and Emergency Communications

The AERO 100 outline splits this unit into contingency planning, ground emergencies, in-flight emergencies, and emergency communications, and the split is a useful teaching frame. Contingency planning happens before the battery is connected: the crew briefing required as part of the section 107.49 preflight covers roles, lost-link expectations, where the aircraft goes on failsafe, where people must not be, and who does what when the plan breaks. Ground emergencies are the events that never leave the surface, dominated by battery fires but including injuries, propeller strikes during handling, and bystander incidents; the mitigations are the handling discipline below plus a first aid kit, a fire container, and a phone that works at the site. In-flight emergencies are the link, GPS, and flyaway events covered in the following sections. [1](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](../sources/ac-107-2a.md)

Emergency communications is the plan for who gets told, and how, when something goes wrong. Within the crew, brief the exact words: a called "abort" or "land now" from a visual observer must trigger action, not discussion. Beyond the crew, the plan names the numbers and channels that matter at that site: 911 for injuries and fires, the airport or ATC facility phone when an errant aircraft threatens traffic near a field (a remote pilot monitoring the CTAF as described in [Radio Communications](radio-communications.md) can hear whether traffic is inbound, but Part 107 pilots do not transmit blind warnings on aviation frequencies as a first resort), and the property owner or client contact. Afterward come the reporting duties: section 107.9 accident reports to the FAA within 10 days for serious injury or qualifying property damage, and NTSB notification under 49 CFR 830 for the most serious events. Writing the site-specific version of this plan is a natural field lab exercise. [1](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](../sources/ac-107-2a.md)

## Lithium Batteries

Lithium-based batteries power nearly every small UAS and are the program's most serious ground hazard. Damaged, overcharged, or shorted cells can enter thermal runaway, a self-sustaining fire that reignites and resists ordinary extinguishing. Safe practice spans the battery's whole life: inspect for swelling or damage before charging and after any hard landing, charge on non-flammable surfaces away from combustibles with someone present, store and transport at moderate charge in fire-resistant containers, and remove a suspect battery from service entirely. The FAA's Safety Alerts for Operators on lithium battery fires and transport are the underlying references, and the ACS tests safe transportation, charging, usage, and fire risk as distinct knowledge elements. This maps directly to the battery safety lecture block planned for AERO 100. [1](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](../sources/ac-107-2a.md)

## Link, GPS, and Spectrum

Loss of the control link is the characteristic drone emergency. The remote pilot must know the aircraft's programmed lost-link behavior (typically return-to-home at a set altitude) and verify its settings, including the home point and RTH altitude against surrounding obstacles, during preflight. A flyaway, where the aircraft departs controlled flight entirely, is the escalation case; the plan is knowing last position, heading, and endurance, and warning anyone at risk, which is why emergency communication planning appears in the ACS. Loss or degradation of GPS removes position hold and geofencing and may shift the aircraft into a manual stabilization mode mid-flight, so students should practice attitude-mode flying before they need it. [1](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](../sources/ac-107-2a.md)

Control links live in shared unlicensed spectrum, typically 2.4 and 5.8 GHz, where distance, obstructions, and other transmitters degrade the link, a subject covered in depth in [Drone Radio Links and Interference](drone-radio-links-and-interference.md). Operating near dense Wi-Fi, large crowds, or transmission infrastructure raises interference risk, which the ACS covers as frequency spectrum limitations. Night and over-people operations add their own contingencies, covered under [Night Operations](night-operations.md) and [Operations Over People](../regulations/operations-over-people.md). [1](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

# Flying Near Energized Infrastructure

**Flying Near Energized Infrastructure** is the set of hazards and work practices that apply when a drone operates close to conductors, towers, and substation equipment that remain energized during the flight. It is the environment behind the inspection work described in [Infrastructure Inspection Operations](infrastructure-inspection-operations.md), and it is unusual among drone operating environments because it attacks the aircraft's sensing systems directly. The magnetic field around a loaded conductor can corrupt the compass, steel lattice structures block and reflect GPS signals, and the obstacles themselves, thin wires strung through open air, are among the hardest objects for either a camera or an obstacle sensor to see. Surrounding all of this is a utility safety culture with legally mandated briefing and clearance rules that a drone crew works inside, not around.

The hazards here are real but frequently garbled in pilot folklore, so this page separates what is documented from what is repeated. Field testing by the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) has shown that modern inspection drones tolerate the electromagnetic environment near energized lines at working distances [9], and specialized aircraft have operated within about ten feet of energized transmission conductors [10]. The documented failures cluster around three specific mechanisms, compass corruption, GPS degradation, and wire strikes, each of which has concrete mitigations.

## Magnetic fields and the compass

A current-carrying conductor is wrapped in a magnetic field whose strength scales with the current on the line, while the line's voltage sets the electric field around it. Both fall off rapidly with distance, which is why modeling studies of extra-high voltage lines frame drone safety as a standoff distance problem [8]. A drone's flight controller estimates heading by comparing its magnetometer reading against the Earth's weak magnetic field, so a strong local field from a loaded conductor, or from the steel of a tower, can swamp the signal the compass depends on. Manufacturers acknowledge this directly. DJI's flight manuals instruct pilots to keep the aircraft away from tall and large metal structures because they affect the accuracy of the onboard compass and satellite positioning, and to minimize interference by avoiding power lines, substations, and broadcast towers [6].

What corrupted heading does to the aircraft depends on how wrong it is. A flight controller that believes north is somewhere it is not will fight its own GPS position corrections, producing the circling drift pilots call toilet-bowling, and in the worst case a flyaway. Industry incident reporting attributes flyaways and sudden loss of control near energized infrastructure to exactly this combination of compass and GPS failure [10]. FirstEnergy, an eastern utility, puts it plainly in its public guidance, stating that electricity causes magnetic interference which can lead a drone to lose control and crash [11]. When the flight controller detects compass interference it typically stops trusting the compass rather than crashing. DJI aircraft automatically drop into Attitude (ATTI) mode when the satellite signal is weak or the compass experiences interference, and in ATTI mode the aircraft no longer holds position and drifts with the wind [6]. An inspection pilot therefore treats ATTI flight as an expected contingency, not an emergency, and practices flying without position hold before working near lines. [Emergency Procedures](emergency-procedures.md) covers the general response to position loss and flyaways.

Calibration is the other half of the mitigation. Compass calibration teaches the aircraft what the local magnetic field looks like, so calibrating in a corrupted field bakes the corruption into every subsequent heading estimate. DJI manuals warn against calibrating anywhere with strong magnetic interference, near massive metal objects, parking structures, or steel reinforcement, and against carrying ferromagnetic items like keys or phones during calibration [7]. In utility work that means calibrating away from the line and the truck, or better, calibrating only when the aircraft requests it and refusing to take off with an active compass warning. The remaining mitigations are geometric. Field strength falls quickly with distance, so pilots hold standoff and use zoom instead of proximity, approach a structure from the side rather than flying along the conductors, and cross a corridor above the structure rather than mid-span, the same rule helicopter patrol crews follow so that every wire stays below the aircraft [12].

## GPS degradation near steel structures

A lattice steel tower degrades satellite positioning two ways. The structure masks part of the sky, cutting the number of satellites the receiver can track, and its steel reflects signals so the receiver also processes delayed copies of what it does receive, the error source called multipath. [GNSS Fundamentals](gnss-fundamentals.md) explains the underlying geometry. The effect is well documented for flight near large structures. Research on drone inspection of bridge substructures maps shadowed zones where satellite navigation degrades or fails outright and plans flight paths around them [16], and the same masking and reflection happens beside and below a transmission tower. Skydio, whose utility inspection aircraft navigate visually, describes close-in transmission work as a GPS-denied condition and treats onboard vision rather than satellite positioning as the primary navigation source there [9].

To the pilot, degradation shows up as a falling satellite count, a position that jumps or slides, or an unprompted switch to ATTI mode. The practical consequences concentrate in automated behaviors. Return-to-home is unreliable if the home point was recorded with a corrupted position or if the aircraft loses positioning en route, and DJI's own manual cautions that the aircraft may enter ATTI mode during a failsafe return and warns pilots to watch for fine objects such as power lines during any return-to-home [6]. Inspection pilots set the return altitude above the tallest structure in the work area, expect to disable or override automation near the tower, and keep enough manual proficiency to fly the aircraft home without position hold.

## Minimum approach distances

Utility work around energized parts is governed by minimum approach distances, or MADs, the closest an unprotected worker or conductive object may come to an energized conductor. The federal rule is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.269, which permits only qualified employees to work within reach of energized parts and sets MADs by voltage [1]. The distance has two components, an electrical component sized so that the air gap will not flash over even during a transient overvoltage, and an inadvertent movement component that allows for human error [4]. The published tables run from avoid contact below 300 volts, through roughly two and a half feet phase-to-ground for distribution circuits in the 15 to 25 kV class, to about 3 feet at 72.5 kV, 3.7 feet at 121 kV, around 11 feet in the 345 kV class, and over 16 feet at 550 kV [2] [3]. For unqualified persons the rule is simpler and much more conservative. OSHA 1910.333 requires an unqualified person to keep themselves and any conductive object at least 10 feet from overhead lines at 50 kV or below, adding 4 inches for every 10 kV above that [5].

Drones sit awkwardly in this framework because the tables were written for workers and the conductive objects they handle, not for aircraft. Utilities resolve this in their own UAS procedures, and the practical numbers span a wide range depending on aircraft, voltage, and program maturity. EPRI field testing confirmed stable flight in energized transmission environments at close working distances [9], and purpose-built aircraft have inspected within about ten feet of energized conductors [10], while the same utilities tell the general public to keep hobby drones at least 200 feet from lines and equipment [11]. A teaching rule of thumb consistent with these sources is that the unqualified person clearance of ten feet plus voltage adder is a floor a drone should never test, that program standoffs are typically several times larger with optical zoom closing the remaining distance, and that the exact number on a given job comes from the utility's procedure, not the pilot's judgment. At distribution voltages the electrical clearances are small and the binding constraint is wire strike. At transmission voltages the electrical clearance itself becomes substantial, and modeling of 345 kV and above frames field exposure as an additional reason to stand off [8].

## Induced voltage, static, and flashover

The electric field around a high-voltage conductor will induce charge on conductive objects in it, which is why utility barehand crews bond to the conductor rather than touch it at a different potential. For a small drone the documented concern is not the field itself at working distances. EPRI testing found no effect on flight behavior, collision avoidance, or command and control at inspection distances [9]. The hard boundary is flashover. Close enough to a conductor, the aircraft becomes part of a conductive path across the air gap, and the electrical component of the MAD exists precisely to keep objects outside the distance a transient overvoltage could arc [4]. A drone that contacts or arcs to a conductor is a lost aircraft at best, and at worst a fault on the line, an outage, or an ignition source in fire country, which is why utility programs treat the MAD as inviolable even for an expendable aircraft [1]. Claims that lines routinely fry drones from tens of feet away are folklore. The documented interference mechanisms are the compass and GPS effects above, and they degrade control rather than electrocute the aircraft.

## Wire strike and corridor geometry

The most likely accident near a line is not electrical at all. It is flying into a wire. A transmission corridor holds more wires than the obvious phase conductors. Shield wires, also called static wires, run along the very top of the structures to intercept lightning, and they are thinner than the conductors and notoriously hard to see. A fatal 2009 helicopter accident during power line inspection practice turned on exactly this, the crew struck the small-diameter shield wires above the conductors they were watching, and the surviving pilot noted the standard procedure they had skipped, crossing the line directly above a structure so all wires stay below the aircraft [12]. Distribution poles add their own trap in guy wires, the anchored steel cables that brace poles and towers, which approach the ground at an angle a pilot scanning for horizontal wires will miss. A drone camera compresses depth along its viewing axis, so a wire between the aircraft and the subject can be invisible on screen, and manufacturer obstacle avoidance does not reliably detect thin wires. DJI's manuals specifically warn pilots to watch for small or fine objects such as power lines that the vision systems cannot handle [6].

The wires also move. Conductor sag increases as the conductor heats, whether from load or from a hot afternoon, dropping the low point of the span by many feet relative to a cool morning [14]. Wind pushes spans sideways, a displacement called blowout that tilts the whole sag profile out of the vertical plane [13]. A position that cleared the conductor on the survey flight may not clear it under afternoon load and wind. Crews manage this with geometry. The pilot and visual observer position themselves so the span is viewed from the side, in profile against the sky, where the gap between aircraft and wire is actually visible, rather than looking down the line where depth is unreadable. Flight paths stay on one side of the corridor, never threading between phases, and cross only above structures.

## Working inside utility safety culture

A drone crew on utility property works under the same safety framework as the line crew. OSHA 1910.269 requires the employee in charge to hold a job briefing before the work starts, covering the hazards, the work procedures, any special precautions, energy source controls, and personal protective equipment, with additional briefings whenever the work changes significantly [1]. In utility practice this briefing is called a tailboard, and it is a participatory discussion where every crew member hears the plan, the hazards, and their own assignment [15]. Many utilities pair the tailboard with a written job hazard analysis that walks each task step against its hazards and controls. A drone pilot joins the tailboard, briefs the flight plan as part of it, and stops work for a new briefing when conditions change, exactly as a lineworker would.

The second cultural fact is status. A Part 107 certificate does not make a pilot a qualified electrical worker, so a drone pilot on a utility job is an unqualified person under OSHA's definitions, bound by the 10 foot plus voltage adder clearance for themselves and everything conductive they handle [5]. The distinction matters most in the small decisions, where to park, where to set the launch point, whether a downed wire nearby is assumed dead. Utility culture assumes every conductor is energized until tested and grounded, and expects unqualified personnel to take direction from the qualified crew rather than reason about the electrical system themselves. FirstEnergy's public guidance draws the same line, noting that its own pilots are trained for these situations and warning everyone else to keep their distance, and that a drone downed inside a substation or against a line is retrieved by the utility, never by its owner [11]. The habits in [Field Operations Best Practices](field-operations-best-practices.md) carry over directly, but on utility work they run inside the utility's briefing, clearance, and stop-work structure.

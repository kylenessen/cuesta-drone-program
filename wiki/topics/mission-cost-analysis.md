# Mission Cost Analysis

**Mission Cost Analysis** is the discipline of understanding what a drone mapping job actually costs before quoting it. New operators tend to price the flight, because the flight is the visible part. In reality the airborne minutes are often the smallest line item. A realistic cost model counts every hour from the first client call to the delivered file, plus the fixed costs of staying in business, and it is the difference between a mapping operation that survives and one that quietly subsidizes its clients.

## Travel and mobilization usually dominate

For small and mid sized jobs, getting a qualified crew to the site is commonly the largest cost. A one hour mapping flight at a site two hours away consumes at least five hours of labor plus vehicle costs before any processing begins. Mobilization also includes the unglamorous overhead of a specific job, airspace authorization, site access coordination, weather delays and refly risk. This is why per acre prices fall steeply with site size and why industry guides emphasize minimum job fees. The mobilization cost of a 5 acre site and a 100 acre site are nearly identical, so the small site costs far more per acre [1] [2].

## Field time versus processing time

Field time is bounded and visible. Processing time is open ended and mostly invisible to the client. A routine site might process overnight with little intervention, but ground control measurement, quality checking, classification cleanup, vector digitizing, volume reporting, and revisions are skilled desk labor. Many practitioners find office hours equal or exceed field hours once deliverables go beyond a raw orthomosaic. Estimating a job therefore means walking the whole pipeline. Plan the mission and handle permissions, travel out and back, set and survey control, fly, ingest and process, perform QA against checkpoints, produce deliverables, and handle delivery and client questions. Assigning honest hours to each stage, then applying a loaded labor rate, produces a defensible floor for the quote.

## Equipment, software, insurance, and overhead

The visible gear is only part of fixed cost. A professional kit includes the aircraft, spare batteries and parts, GNSS equipment for control, targets, a capable processing computer, and software subscriptions that commonly run thousands of dollars per year at the professional tier. Aircraft and sensors should be amortized over a realistic service life measured in a few years, not treated as one time purchases, because crashes, battery aging, and platform obsolescence are normal. On top of that sit business overhead items, licensing and training currency, marketing, accounting, and unbillable time. Solo operators frequently discover that only a fraction of their working hours are billable, which is why sustainable day rates look high next to hobbyist expectations [2].

## Insurance and liability basics

The FAA does not require insurance for Part 107 operations, but commercial clients routinely do, and flying commercial work uninsured is a business risk few professionals accept [3] [4]. The core product is aviation liability coverage, which pays for injury or property damage the operation causes to third parties. One million dollars per occurrence is widely described as the practical industry baseline, with some construction and government clients requiring two million or more, and annual policies for that level are commonly reported in the several hundred to roughly fifteen hundred dollar range depending on operation and equipment [3] [4]. Hull coverage for the aircraft itself is separate and optional. On demand per flight policies exist for occasional work. Clients often require being named as an additional insured, and a certificate of insurance is standard paperwork on commercial contracts. Standard business concerns, contracts that define deliverables and accuracy claims, and disclaimers where work borders licensed surveying, are part of the same risk picture.

## Pricing approaches in the industry

Published guidance describes several coexisting models. Time based pricing, hourly or day rate, fits variable or exploratory work. Per acre or per site pricing fits repeatable mapping products, with published per acre figures varying widely by deliverable and accuracy, and lidar commanding several times the price of RGB photogrammetry [1] [2] [5]. Per deliverable and subscription pricing fits recurring programs such as monthly construction progress mapping, where the client buys an outcome rather than hours. Whatever the model, the quote should be reconciled against the bottom up hour estimate. If the market price for a job is below your modeled cost, the correct response is usually to decline or restructure the job, not to fly it at a loss.

## When drone mapping is not the cheap option

Drones earned a reputation as the low cost disruptor, and often they are, but not always. Very large areas favor manned aircraft or satellites on cost per acre once mobilization is spread over enough ground, with industry guides placing the crossover in the hundreds to low thousands of acres [1]. Very small questions may not need new imagery at all if public orthoimagery or a tape measure answers them. Heavily vegetated terrain may require lidar, at a substantially higher price, or make photogrammetry outputs worthless for the client's real question. Repeat monitoring programs can also be cheaper per epoch with fixed sensors or periodic satellite tasking than with a crew mobilized monthly. Part of professional cost analysis is telling the client when the drone is the wrong buy. That honesty tends to return as trust and repeat work.

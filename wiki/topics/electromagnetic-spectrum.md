# Electromagnetic Spectrum

The **electromagnetic spectrum** is the continuous range of light across every wavelength, of which visible color occupies only a very narrow band. For advanced drone applications this matters because cameras can be built to see beyond what the human eye detects, and different wavelengths reveal different things about the world. A short tour of the spectrum pays off repeatedly across the course. [1]

## Visible Light Is a Narrow Band

What people call color is a small slice of a much longer continuous stretch of wavelengths. A camera can, in principle, respond to light well outside that slice. In practice, capturing or excluding specific wavelengths is an engineering decision, made with special pieces of glass that filter which wavelengths reach the photosensitive part of the sensor and which are blocked. Choosing what a sensor can and cannot see is central to building instruments for specific jobs, and it is the same filtering that produces color, as described in [Digital Imaging](digital-imaging.md). [1]

## Infrared

Just beyond red light is infrared, the most useful region for drone work after visible light. Its key property is the ability to distinguish photosynthetic material from non-photosynthetic material, which makes near-infrared the foundation of vegetation analysis. The applications of infrared reflectance, including the red edge and NDVI, are covered in [Multispectral Imaging](multispectral-imaging.md). [1] Much further from the visible band sits longwave infrared, which surfaces emit as a function of their own temperature rather than reflect from the sun. Cameras that image this emitted radiation, and the inspection work built on them, are covered in [Thermal Imaging](thermal-imaging.md).

## Ultraviolet

Ultraviolet light sits on the other side of the visible band, at shorter wavelengths than violet, and has comparatively few drone applications today. It is not, however, without genuine and teachable uses. On drones the leading application is corona-discharge inspection of power lines with solar-blind UV cameras, and off the aircraft ultraviolet imaging is a workhorse in forensics and in the ecology of flowers and pollinators. Those applications, and why UV imaging is harder to build than a visible camera, are treated in [Ultraviolet Imaging](ultraviolet-imaging.md). [1]

## A Note on the Prefixes

The infra and ultra prefixes are Latin, and once translated they resolve the confusion that spectrum graphics can create. Infra means below or beneath, and ultra means beyond, so infrared literally names the light "below red" and ultraviolet the light "beyond violet." [2][3] The direction refers to frequency and energy rather than to a diagram's left-to-right layout: infrared lies just below red in frequency, at longer wavelengths, while ultraviolet lies just beyond violet, at higher frequency and shorter wavelength. The etymology is not merely tidy, it is literal history. William Herschel discovered infrared in 1800 by finding a temperature rise just beyond the red end of a spectrum cast by a prism, and Johann Ritter discovered ultraviolet in 1801 by finding that light past the violet end darkened photographic paper. [2][3] Each prefix marks the region immediately outside its end of visible light, exactly as the discoverers found it.

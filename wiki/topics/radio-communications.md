# Radio Communications

**Radio Communications** for a remote pilot is a listening skill: Part 107 does not require radio use, but monitoring the frequencies manned pilots use is the most effective way to know where traffic is before it becomes visible. The ACS makes radio procedures its first Operations task, testing the Common Traffic Advisory Frequency, UNICOM, ATIS, call signs, the phonetic alphabet, and standard phraseology, all so the remote pilot can decode what they hear. [1](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

## Frequencies and Services

The Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF) is the designated frequency for traffic advisories at an airport without an operating control tower. Manned pilots self-announce position and intentions on it (taxiing, entering downwind, final approach), and a remote pilot with an inexpensive aviation-band receiver can assemble a mental picture of the pattern from those calls. UNICOM is an aeronautical advisory station, often run by the airport operator or FBO, which may share the CTAF frequency and provides airport information on request. At towered airports, the Automatic Terminal Information Service (ATIS) broadcasts a recorded loop of current weather and airport conditions, updated as conditions change and labeled with a phonetic letter. The CTAF, UNICOM, and ATIS frequencies for a field are printed on the [Sectional Chart](/topics/sectional-chart.md) and in the Chart Supplement. [1](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

## Understanding What You Hear

Aircraft identify themselves by call sign built from the registration number, spoken with the ICAO phonetic alphabet (Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, and so on), which students should memorize outright since exam questions and real traffic calls both use it. Phraseology conventions matter for comprehension: altitudes are spoken as thousands and hundreds of feet MSL, directions are magnetic headings spoken as three digits, speeds are knots, and time is Zulu (UTC). A typical self-announcement, such as a pilot reporting entering left downwind for runway 29, tells a listening remote pilot the aircraft's position, its next two turns, and where it will be in ninety seconds, which is exactly the situational awareness the FAA wants near airports as described in [Airport Operations](/topics/airport-operations.md). [2](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

A good lab exercise is playing recorded CTAF traffic and having students plot each aircraft on an airport diagram, converting listening into the traffic picture the test's scenario questions assume.

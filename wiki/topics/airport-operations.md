# Airport Operations

**Airport Operations** covers what a remote pilot must understand about airports to operate safely in their vicinity: the types of airports, how manned traffic flows around them, what the markings and signage mean, and where airport data lives. Drones under Part 107 may operate near airports (in Class G, no authorization is needed even close to a nontowered field), so the FAA's emphasis is awareness and yielding rather than avoidance, and the [Part 107 Knowledge Test](/exams/part-107-knowledge-test.md) tests this material heavily within its largest content area. [1](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

## Airports and Traffic Flow

Airports range from towered fields, where ATC directs traffic and operations in the surrounding Class B, C, or D airspace require [Airspace Authorization](/topics/airspace-authorization.md), to nontowered fields where pilots coordinate themselves over a common frequency, plus heliports and seaplane bases with their own patterns. Manned traffic at nontowered airports typically flies a left-hand rectangular traffic pattern at roughly 1,000 feet above field elevation, entering on a 45-degree leg to downwind, so the highest collision risk zones for a drone are the approach and departure corridors aligned with the runway. Runways are numbered by magnetic direction rounded to the nearest 10 degrees (runway 29 points near 290 degrees magnetic), which lets a remote pilot infer where traffic will appear once they know the wind. [2](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

A remote pilot near an airport should monitor the field's frequency to build a picture of who is where, as covered in [Radio Communications](/topics/radio-communications.md), and must never interfere with traffic. Right of way always belongs to manned aircraft under the [Part 107 Operating Rules](/regulations/part-107-operating-rules.md). [1](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

## Markings, Signage, and Hazards

Test items include runway holding position markings (four yellow lines, two solid and two dashed, where the solid side means hold), runway and taxiway signage, and airport lighting such as the rotating beacon, runway edge lights, and visual glideslope indicators, all of which help a remote pilot interpret what manned aircraft are doing, especially at night. Security Identification Display Areas (SIDA) are portions of an airport where unescorted access requires security clearance, relevant to commercial pilots hired for on-airport work. Wildlife is a shared hazard: bird strikes are reportable through the FAA's wildlife strike program, and drone operations should avoid concentrating birds toward runways. [1](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [2](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

Airport data lives in two places: the [Sectional Chart](/topics/sectional-chart.md) for location, elevation, airspace, and primary frequencies, and the Chart Supplement for the full directory entry including hours, all frequencies, and remarks. Exam questions probe which document answers which question. [1](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

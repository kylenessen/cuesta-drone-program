# Drone Mapping and Licensed Surveying

**Drone Mapping and Licensed Surveying** overlap in tools and outputs but are legally distinct activities, and the distinction carries real enforcement risk. Land surveying is a licensed profession regulated state by state. A Part 107 certificate authorizes flying for compensation. It says nothing about what services the resulting data may lawfully support. Several state boards have pursued drone operators for unlicensed surveying, so a professional mapper needs a working understanding of where the line sits, especially in their own state. Nothing here is legal advice. It is background for understanding a regulatory landscape that is still evolving.

## The core distinction

The heart of licensed surveying is authoritative determination, above all of property boundaries. Locating, establishing, or retracing a property line, setting monuments, and certifying positions that others may rely on legally are the practice of land surveying essentially everywhere in the United States. A drone mapper who overlays approximate parcel lines from county GIS onto an orthomosaic and presents them as where the boundary is has crossed into that territory. Mapping, by contrast, produces representations, imagery, elevation models, and measurements offered without authoritative certification. Commentators commonly summarize the difference as surveying being subject to licensure while mapping is not, but the statutes, not the labels, control, and statutes in some states sweep more broadly than the boundary question [4] [5].

## California and the Professional Land Surveyors' Act

In California, land surveying is governed by the Professional Land Surveyors' Act, Business and Professions Code sections 8700 and following, administered by the Board for Professional Engineers, Land Surveyors, and Geologists, known as BPELSG [1]. Section 8726 defines the practice broadly. It includes the expected boundary work, such as locating, relocating, establishing, reestablishing, or retracing any property line or boundary, and setting monuments [2]. Critically for drone operators, it also includes determining "the configuration or contour of the earth's surface, or the position of fixed objects above, on, or below the surface of the earth by applying the principles of mathematics or photogrammetry" [2]. Read literally, that language reaches core photogrammetric mapping products such as topographic surfaces and contours, not just boundary work. How far the Board presses that reach in practice, and what exemptions or interpretations apply to specific products, are questions a California drone mapping business should put to BPELSG or a licensed attorney directly rather than settle from secondary sources. The Act's text is published by the Board and is the authoritative reference [1].

## Enforcement is real

This is not a theoretical concern. State boards in North Carolina, Mississippi, California, and elsewhere have warned or acted against drone operators over unlicensed surveying claims [4]. The most prominent recent case is 360 Virtual Drone Services LLC v. Ritter, in which a North Carolina drone operator argued the state's surveying licensure law violated his First Amendment rights by restricting his sale of maps and 3D models. The Fourth Circuit ruled for the surveying board in May 2024, holding the licensing regime regulates professional conduct and survives constitutional scrutiny, and the Supreme Court declined to take the case [3]. In Mississippi, by contrast, Vizaline, a company drawing approximate property lines from deed descriptions, prevailed against that state's board after First Amendment litigation [4]. The mixed outcomes underline that the boundary is state specific and contested, which argues for conservatism in how services are described and sold.

## Working under or alongside licensed surveyors

The clean solution for work near the line is a licensed surveyor in responsible charge. In this arrangement the drone operator functions as a data acquisition technician. The surveyor defines the control and accuracy requirements, reviews the products, and signs the deliverables, taking professional responsibility for them. Many survey and engineering firms now employ or subcontract drone pilots on exactly these terms, and it is a substantial employment path for trained mapping pilots. Independent mappers serving such firms should expect the surveyor to dictate datums, control, and QA, and should welcome that structure, since it transfers the professional liability to the license holder. Outside that arrangement, mappers stay safest with products and language that make no authoritative claims, imagery for visualization, models for planning, volumes for internal inventory, and by declining any request to show, mark, or certify a boundary.

## Disclaimer language and professional practice

Non-survey deliverables commonly carry explicit disclaimers, and drone law commentators recommend them, particularly when any approximate line appears on an image. One published example states that depicted property lines are approximations for visualization only, are not based on a survey by a licensed surveyor, and must not be used in place of one [5]. Avoiding the word survey in marketing and deliverables unless a licensee is involved is likewise widely advised [5]. A disclaimer is not a shield against a statute that regulates the underlying activity, so it complements, rather than replaces, staying on the right side of the definition. For a curriculum, the durable lesson is that students entering mapping work should learn their state's definition of surveying, know their board, and treat the boundary question as a referral to a licensed professional, not a product line.

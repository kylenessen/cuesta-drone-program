# NDAA Compliance and Blue UAS

**NDAA Compliance and Blue UAS** are the two terms that come up whenever government money touches a drone project. They are related but not interchangeable. NDAA compliance is a supply chain standard rooted in federal statute. Blue UAS is a Department of Defense vetting program that goes further. Both exist because Congress and the Pentagon decided that drones made in China, above all DJI aircraft, pose an unacceptable data security risk for government work. For anyone flying on federally funded contracts, these rules now decide what aircraft can be on the truck.

## What NDAA compliance means

The foundation is Section 848 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2020, signed in December 2019 and later codified at 10 U.S.C. 4881. It prohibits the Department of Defense from operating or procuring a covered unmanned aircraft system manufactured in a covered foreign country, defined to include China, or built with critical components from one [1]. The critical components list covers flight controllers, radios and data transmission devices, cameras, gimbals, ground control systems, and operating software. A drone is commonly called NDAA compliant when the manufacturer attests that none of those components come from a covered country. There is no certification body for plain NDAA compliance. It is a self declaration.

The American Security Drone Act of 2023, enacted as part of the FY2024 NDAA, extended these restrictions well beyond the Pentagon. It bars federal agencies from procuring covered drones, and it prohibits the use of federal funds awarded through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements to purchase or operate them [2]. The implementing FAR rule took effect in late 2024 [3], and as of December 22, 2025 federal agencies are also prohibited from operating covered aircraft they already own [2]. Separately, the FCC added foreign produced UAS and UAS critical components to its Covered List on December 22, 2025, after no agency completed the security review required by Section 1709 of the FY2025 NDAA. That action blocks new equipment authorizations, which means new DJI models cannot legally be imported or marketed in the United States, though previously authorized models may still be sold and flown [4].

## What Blue UAS adds

The Blue UAS Cleared List began at the Defense Innovation Unit as a catalog of small drones vetted for DoD use. Clearance requires more than an NDAA supply chain attestation. Platforms go through cybersecurity evaluation and policy compliance verification, so a Blue listed aircraft is presumed safe for defense procurement without further approval paperwork. Every Blue UAS platform is NDAA compliant, but most NDAA compliant drones are not Blue listed. Following a July 2025 Secretary of War memo, management of the Cleared List transitioned from DIU to the Defense Contract Management Agency, and the current list is published at bluelist.dcma.mil [5].

## Who is actually bound

Federal agencies are bound directly. Contractors and grant recipients are bound whenever federal dollars pay for the drone work. That last part is what reaches local practice. The Morro Bay National Estuary Program, part of the EPA funded National Estuary Program [6], cannot pay contractors who fly DJI aircraft when the money is federal. A mapping contractor in San Luis Obispo County who only owns DJI equipment is simply ineligible for that work. Several states have gone further on their own. Florida requires government agencies to buy only from an approved manufacturer list maintained by its Department of Management Services, which excludes DJI and Autel and grounded existing fleets in 2023 [7]. Purely private commercial work under Part 107 remains unrestricted, for now.

The funding-source distinction runs straight through this program's own purchasing, as laid out in the program's April 2026 cost planning correspondence [10]. Perkins funds are federal money awarded under the Strengthening Career and Technical Education for the 21st Century Act, so the American Security Drone Act binds them directly: Perkins-funded drone purchases must be NDAA compliant, and DJI and Autel are ineligible under that stream. Strong Workforce Program funds come from the California state budget, not a federal grant, so the ASDA imposes no legal mandate on them, which is why the college's existing DJI Mavic 3E and 3T, bought with prior SWP money, remain usable for instruction. The program nonetheless plans NDAA compliant platforms for all new purchases regardless of funding source. That is strategy rather than obligation: the utilities, public safety agencies, and government employers in its primary hiring sectors, PG&E and Cal OES among them, have mandated or are actively transitioning to compliant fleets, and training students on aircraft their future employers cannot fly would disadvantage graduates. Buying compliant also keeps every purchase eligible under every funding stream as the regulatory environment continues to tighten [10].

## The compliant platform landscape

The Cleared List changes often, so verify current membership at the official site before quoting it [5]. As of mid 2026 the list includes over fifty platforms from more than thirty manufacturers. Names relevant to mapping work include Skydio (X10D), Inspired Flight (IF800 Tomcat and IF1200A), Freefly Systems (Astro and Alta X Blue), Wingtra (WingtraOne Gen II and WingtraRAY fixed wing mapping aircraft), Parrot (ANAFI USA GOV/MIL), Ascent AeroSystems, Vantage Robotics, and Teal [8]. Inspired Flight is headquartered in San Luis Obispo, which makes it a natural local industry connection for this program.

The cost and capability gap versus DJI is real and worth stating plainly. A DJI Matrice 350 RTK airframe runs roughly ten to twelve thousand dollars and pairs with a mature payload ecosystem for photogrammetry, LiDAR, and thermal work. Comparable compliant setups cost more. Skydio X10 packages commonly land in the fifteen to twenty five thousand dollar range, and an Inspired Flight IF800 with a mapping payload such as the Sony LR1 can reach the forties [9]. Endurance, payload variety, and software polish still generally favor DJI. The gap has narrowed each year, and for federally funded work the comparison is moot anyway. The compliant aircraft is the only aircraft that can fly the job.

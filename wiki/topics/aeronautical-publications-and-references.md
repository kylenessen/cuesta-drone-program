# Aeronautical Publications and References

**Aeronautical Publications and References** is the remote pilot's reference shelf: the regulations themselves, the FAA's advisory and procedural publications, the Chart Supplement directory, and the media that keep a working pilot current after the course ends. The AERO 100 outline's final unit, Regulations, Procedures, and Other Aeronautical References, is about knowing which document answers which question, a skill the [Part 107 Knowledge Test](../exams/part-107-knowledge-test.md) probes by asking where a given fact is published. ## Regulations and Procedures Publications

The binding rules live in the Code of Federal Regulations. Title 14 covers aeronautics and space, and the parts a remote pilot touches most are [Part 107](../regulations/part-107.md) itself, the Part 47 and Part 48 registration rules behind [Drone Registration](../regulations/drone-registration.md), and Part 89 [Remote Identification](../regulations/remote-identification.md). AC 107-2A's own reference list adds Part 91 general operating rules, Title 47 Part 87 on aviation radio services, and Title 49 Part 830, the NTSB accident notification rule that operates alongside the FAA's section 107.9 reporting duty. The authoritative current text of any of these is the eCFR at ecfr.gov, which matters because printed and third-party copies drift out of date. [3](../sources/ac-107-2a.md)

Alongside the regulations sits the Aeronautical Information Manual (AIM), the FAA's official guide to basic flight information and air traffic control procedures. The AIM is not regulatory, but it is the reference manned pilots fly by, and it is where a remote pilot looks up procedures the drone rules assume, such as traffic pattern conventions and light gun signals. The FAA also publishes handbooks (the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge is the deep version of most knowledge test subjects), the Airman Certification Standards that define every tested element, and the Remote Pilot Study Guide written for this certificate. Nearly all of it is downloadable free from faa.gov. [1](../sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

## Advisory Circulars

Advisory Circulars are the FAA's method of publishing guidance: acceptable, but usually not the only, means of complying with a regulation. They are numbered by subject to parallel the CFR, which is why the small UAS circular is AC 107-2 (currently AC 107-2A), matching Part 107. An AC does not create binding rules, but ignoring one means inventing your own compliance argument, so in practice ACs function as the operating manual for the regulation they accompany. The ones a remote pilot should know by name are AC 107-2A itself, AC 00-6 Aviation Weather, and AC 00-45 Aviation Weather Services, the latter two being the FAA's full weather references behind the material in [Weather Effects on Performance](weather-effects-on-performance.md). Current editions are on the FAA's Advisory Circulars page, and checking for the current edition is part of using them. [3](../sources/ac-107-2a.md)

## The Chart Supplement

The Chart Supplement U.S., formerly the Airport/Facility Directory, is the most comprehensive published source of airport data, covering every public-use airport, heliport, and seaplane base: hours of operation, all communication frequencies, runway details, lighting, fuel, services, and free-text remarks that include hazards and parachute jump operations. It is organized in regional volumes revised every 56 days and is available digitally from the FAA's aeronautical information services. The working division of labor: the [Sectional Chart](sectional-chart.md) shows where an airport is, its airspace, and its primary frequencies, while the Chart Supplement holds everything else, and [Airport Operations](airport-operations.md) covers how a remote pilot uses both. Exam questions test exactly this division, asking which document reveals, for example, a field's tower hours or its parachute activity. [1](../sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md) [2](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

## UAS Terms and Abbreviations

Aviation compresses itself into abbreviations, and the drone subset is testable vocabulary: UA and sUAS for the aircraft, UAS for the aircraft plus its control station and links, remote PIC for the pilot in command, VO for visual observer, CS for control station, AGL and MSL for the two altitude references, ATC, NOTAM, TFR, METAR and TAF, LAANC, CoW for certificate of waiver, and DoC for declaration of compliance. The official lists live in the appendices of the ACS and AC 107-2A, which resolve any abbreviation a test question uses. The terminology itself is still moving, with unmanned giving way to uncrewed in much current writing while the regulations retain the older term, a shift discussed in [Uncrewed Aircraft](uncrewed-aircraft.md). [2](../sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [3](../sources/ac-107-2a.md)

## Aviation Media and Staying Current

The certificate outlives the course, and the rules keep moving, so a graduate needs sources that update. The FAA's own channels come first: the FAA UAS website and its email updates, FAA Safety Briefing magazine, which is free and now published quarterly, and the FAA Safety Team (FAASTeam) at faasafety.gov, the site that also hosts the recurrent training that keeps a [Remote Pilot Certificate](../regulations/remote-pilot-certificate.md) current. Industry media, including the general aviation press, drone trade outlets, and manufacturer channels, surface changes faster and cover craft the FAA does not, but they should be verified against primary sources before being relied on, a discipline this wiki itself models. The pending [Part 108 BVLOS Rulemaking](../regulations/part-108-bvlos-rulemaking.md) is the standing example of why the habit matters: students certificated this year will see the operating rules change under them.

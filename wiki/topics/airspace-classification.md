# Airspace Classification

**Airspace Classification** is the system that divides the National Airspace System into classes with different rules, services, and entry requirements. For a remote pilot the operative distinction is controlled versus uncontrolled: operations in Class B, C, or D airspace, or in Class E airspace designated for an airport, require prior authorization under section 107.41, while Class G requires none. Airspace is the second-largest content area on the [Part 107 Knowledge Test](/exams/part-107-knowledge-test.md), usually tested through [Sectional Chart](/topics/sectional-chart.md) excerpts that the student must interpret. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md) [2](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

## Controlled Airspace

Class A runs from 18,000 feet mean sea level upward and is not relevant to small UAS operations, which are capped far below it. Class B surrounds the busiest airports, generally from the surface to 10,000 feet MSL, shaped as individually tailored layers often described as an upside-down wedding cake. Class C surrounds airports with an operational control tower and radar approach control, typically a 5 nautical mile core from the surface to 4,000 feet above airport elevation plus a shelf out to 10 nautical miles from 1,200 to 4,000 feet. Class D surrounds smaller towered airports, generally from the surface to 2,500 feet above airport elevation, individually tailored. Class E is controlled airspace that is not A, B, C, or D; its floor may be the surface (often around airports with instrument approaches), 700 feet AGL under the magenta shading on sectional charts, or 1,200 feet AGL over much of the country. Remote pilots need authorization in Class E only where it is designated for an airport, most commonly Class E to the surface. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

Class G is uncontrolled airspace, everything not designated A through E, extending from the surface up to the base of the overlying Class E. Small UAS operations in Class G need no ATC authorization, which is why most rural and low-altitude drone work proceeds without contact with the FAA. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

## Special Use and Other Airspace

Special use airspace confines activities that must be segregated from other traffic. Prohibited areas ban flight outright; restricted areas contain hazards such as artillery firing and require permission from the using or controlling agency when active; warning areas extend offshore; military operations areas (MOAs) separate military training from IFR traffic; alert areas flag high volumes of training activity; and controlled firing areas suspend their activity when aircraft approach, so they do not appear on charts. [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md) [2](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md)

The ACS also expects familiarity with other designated areas: temporary flight restrictions (TFRs) published by NOTAM, which bind drone operations just as they bind manned aircraft; military training routes (MTRs), flown fast and low, where a three-digit route number signals operations at or below 1,500 feet AGL; terminal radar service areas (TRSAs); national security areas; parachute jump operations areas listed in the Chart Supplement; and VFR routes and corridors. The practical habit to teach is checking NOTAMs and TFRs during preflight planning, since airspace that was open yesterday can be closed today for a wildfire, a stadium event, or a presidential visit. [2](/sources/faa-s-acs-10b.md) [1](/sources/remote-pilot-study-guide.md)

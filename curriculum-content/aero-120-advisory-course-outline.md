# AERO 120: Drone Utility and Infrastructure Inspection

Draft course outline for advisory committee review
Kyle Nessen, July 2026

This course prepares certificated drone pilots for professional utility and infrastructure inspection work. Students arrive having completed AERO 100, so they hold the FAA Remote Pilot Certificate, have demonstrated flight proficiency, and understand core drone operations. AERO 120 builds the specialized layer on top: thermal imaging, precision flight near energized equipment, asset knowledge, and defensible inspection reporting. The course is designed around the region's dominant employer of inspection pilots, Pacific Gas and Electric, and is planned in collaboration with PG&E and IBEW Local 1245. It carries a credential goal modeled on how AERO 100 handles the Part 107 exam. Students study the full Level 1 thermography body of knowledge during the semester so they can complete certification immediately after and enter the job market with the credential that utility, solar, and insurance clients expect. The aim is a graduate who can walk onto an inspection job site on day one and be productive, knowing the equipment, the hazards, the workflow, and what a finding looks like when it matters.

## The utility inspection industry

Why utilities fly drones, and where the jobs are. This unit grounds everything else in the regulatory and employment reality of California utility work.

- California's inspection mandates under CPUC General Orders 95 and 165, the inspection cycles they require, and the wildfire risk that pushed aerial inspection to the center of utility operations
- PG&E's drone program as the regional anchor: roughly 80 pilots in the field daily and more than 300,000 pole and tower inspections flown in 2024
- Where entry-level employment actually sits, including the contractors and service companies that perform much of the flying and image review
- The direction of the industry: dock-based remote operations, beyond visual line of sight waivers, and the shift toward remote operators and data reviewers
- The IBEW 1245 training model and how union and utility qualification ladders sit on top of the Part 107 foundation students already hold

## Thermal imaging and Level 1 thermography preparation

Content covers the Level 1 thermography body of knowledge as published in the industry qualification standards (ASNT SNT-TC-1A and CP-105), so students finish the semester prepared to earn the certification.

- Infrared physics: the electromagnetic spectrum, heat transfer, longwave infrared, and how microbolometer sensors turn radiation into a radiometric image
- Emissivity and reflected temperature: apparent versus true temperature, and the measurement traps that produce confident wrong answers
- Spot size and measurement distance: how flying height determines whether a temperature reading is valid at all
- Camera operation: level and span, palette selection, and the discipline of separating the data from the display
- Hands-on thermal labs with the program's inspection aircraft, building from calibration targets to realistic fault scenarios

## Interpreting and reporting findings

This unit teaches students to turn a thermal or zoom image into a defensible inspection call and a report a client can act on.

- Delta-T severity classification: temperature rise over reference, industry severity tiers, and the repair priorities they drive
- The inspection conditions that make thermal comparisons honest: electrical load, wind, and solar gain
- Distinguishing real anomalies from reflections, soiling, and environmental artifacts
- Report discipline: consistent per-structure documentation, condition rating, and the recordkeeping utility mandates require
- Separating observation from interpretation, and what the pilot documents versus what the client's engineers decide

## Flying the job

The flight environment that makes utility inspection its own discipline, and the reason it stands apart from the mapping course. Mapping is flown by automated mission plans. Inspection is flown by hand, close to hazards, with the camera doing the approaching.

- Zoom-from-standoff technique: filling the frame with a cotter pin or splice from a safe distance rather than flying close
- Flying near energized infrastructure: compass interference, GPS degradation, minimum approach distances, wire strike, and utility safety culture
- Radio link behavior near towers and lines, and the lost-link failsafes that get tested in exactly these environments
- Precision manual flight drills, revisited from AERO 100 at the tighter tolerances inspection demands
- Field discipline for inspection days: job briefings, checklists, attention management, and go/no-go judgment

## Asset classes and adjacent markets

The core techniques sell into several markets beyond the power line. Students focus on electric utility assets and get working exposure to the near neighbors, which widens their employment base and gives incumbent utility workers a view of adjacent career paths.

- Distribution and transmission inspection: poles, towers, insulators, splices, and transformer and substation equipment, with the common failure modes that show up in zoom and thermal imagery
- Solar farm inspection as a full workflow: irradiance and weather requirements, flight geometry, the thermal defect taxonomy from hot cells to string outages, and the module-level anomaly map clients buy
- Survey-level exposure to cell tower, wind turbine, and bridge inspection, and what each adds to the flight environment and the deliverable

## Platform and compliance

The equipment context of utility work, taught on the same class of aircraft the industry flies.

- The dual-payload inspection platform: thermal sensor plus high-zoom visual camera, and why that pairing defines the trade
- NDAA compliance and the Blue UAS list: why platform origin matters to utility and government clients, and how it shapes procurement

## Partnership and instruction

The course is being designed with its employers rather than for them. PG&E's aerial operations team has reviewed the program concept and confirmed the demand for a local pipeline of trained operators. We intend to deepen that collaboration as the course develops.

- Curriculum review by PG&E and IBEW 1245 so course content matches the qualification ladder graduates will enter
- Guest instruction from working utility inspection pilots for the asset knowledge that only comes from the field
- Credit for prior learning pathways in both directions, so completed utility or union training can earn course credit and college coursework can shorten employer onboarding

## Where we want the committee's input

- Does this content match what an entry-level inspection pilot needs on day one, and what is missing?
- Which thermography certification pathway should the course align with, and does the industry have a preference among providers?
- What is the current state of internal utility drone training, and where could a college course plug in most usefully?
- How much time do the adjacent asset classes deserve relative to the electric utility core?
- Who from the field could contribute guest instruction?

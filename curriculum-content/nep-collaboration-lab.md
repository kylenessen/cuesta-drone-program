# NEP Collaboration Lab Activity

This document captures the concept for a collaborative lab partnership between Cuesta's drone program and the Morro Bay National Estuary Program (NEP).

## Background

The NEP previously explored purchasing a drone to support restoration monitoring work. The original proposal involved an MOU with California State Parks where the NEP would own the drone and State Parks staff would operate it. That plan was ultimately set aside, and the drone was instead purchased by Cuesta College to support this drone program. However, the underlying need remains: the NEP has ongoing restoration projects that benefit from aerial imagery for monitoring purposes.

Restoration project monitoring represents an excellent application of drone mapping. These projects require periodic aerial surveys to document site conditions, track vegetation changes, and assess restoration progress over time. This type of work exemplifies what professional drone operators do in environmental and land management contexts.

## Partnership Concept

The core idea is to offer drone mapping services to the NEP through lab exercises in the advanced mapping course. Students would conduct real mapping missions at NEP restoration sites as part of their coursework, producing deliverables that serve actual monitoring needs.

This creates multiple benefits:

For the NEP, mapping services would be provided at no cost for projects that might otherwise require budget allocation for contractor services or go undone entirely. The spatial data products would support grant reporting, site documentation, and restoration planning.

For Cuesta, the partnership demonstrates community engagement and creates tangible program outcomes beyond classroom exercises. It establishes connections between the college and regional environmental organizations, which may lead to employment pathways for graduates.

For students, real-world projects provide experience that simulated exercises cannot match. They generate actual data that people need, work at interesting field sites, and practice the complete workflow of professional drone operations. This is the best kind of training because they are doing the work, not just learning about it.

As a board member of the NEP, this partnership also represents a meaningful contribution to the organization through creating value, reducing costs, and producing useful products.

## Lab Activity Design

This collaboration would fit within the Advanced Drone Mapping course. The activity structure would include a comprehensive pre-flight planning assignment, field execution with hands-on practice, and documentation that mirrors professional deliverable preparation.

### Pre-Flight Planning Assignment

Every student would complete a written planning exercise addressing the full scope of mission preparation. This serves as both an assessment of planning competence and a practical exercise in thoroughness.

The assignment would require students to address airspace verification, including checking for controlled airspace, requesting LAANC authorization if needed, and identifying any temporary flight restrictions. Students should document hazards and obstacles within five miles of the site, such as nearby airports, power lines, and other relevant features.

Flight planning specifics would include a screenshot of the planned polygon with flight path overlay, specification of overlap percentages with justification for those choices, identification of takeoff and landing locations with rationale for selection, planned flight altitude and speed, lane direction and pattern, and expected battery consumption with field charging plans if needed.

Operational planning elements would cover visual observer identification if applicable, weather assessment for the planned flight window, and ground control point locations with justification for placement strategy. Students should also identify permission requirements by researching parcel ownership and determining who grants site access.

A critical component is that students must justify each decision. Why this overlap percentage? Why this altitude? Why this takeoff location? The reasoning behind choices matters as much as the choices themselves, since drone operations involve constant tradeoffs between competing considerations.

This assignment can be graded before field execution, allowing feedback on planning quality. The best-developed plans might be selected for actual execution, or the instructor can synthesize elements from multiple submissions into the final mission plan.

### Field Execution

Depending on class size, field execution may require creative approaches to ensure everyone gains hands-on experience. Options include dividing the survey polygon into sections so each student flies a portion of the total area, rotating through roles so students take turns as pilot in command while others manage ground control point collection or serve as visual observers, and building in repetitive practice for fundamental skills like takeoff, landing, battery changes, and equipment checks.

The field component should give students practice at the mechanical aspects of field operations while also exposing them to the decision-making and situational awareness required in real mapping work. Even brief individual flight segments provide valuable stick time and workflow familiarity.

### Documentation and Deliverables

Students would practice writing the methods section of a technical report documenting the flight. This skill transfers directly to professional contexts where drone data collection is part of larger projects requiring formal documentation, or where standalone mapping work requires deliverable packages for clients.

## Broader Vision

This NEP partnership represents one instance of a larger approach: using real community projects as the primary vehicle for hands-on learning. The goal is building a pipeline where students learn by doing actual work that serves real needs.

This model works because educational programs occupy a unique position. The primary purpose is student training, and real projects provide the best training. As a secondary benefit, partnerships with community organizations create valuable spatial data that might not otherwise exist or would require budget allocation for commercial services.

The program can be selective about which projects to pursue. Not every request needs to be accepted. Projects can be chosen based on educational value, logistical feasibility, and alignment with curriculum objectives. This selectivity distinguishes educational partnerships from commercial service provision.

The question of whether this undercuts commercial drone service providers deserves consideration. In practice, many of the organizations that would benefit from these partnerships have limited budgets and would not otherwise contract for professional mapping services. The students who graduate from this program may go on to serve commercial markets with clients who do have budgets for professional work. The educational partnership model and the commercial service market largely serve different segments.
